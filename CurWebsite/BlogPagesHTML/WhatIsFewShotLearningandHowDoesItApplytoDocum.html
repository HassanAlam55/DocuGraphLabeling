What Is Few-Shot Learning and How Does It Apply to Documents?

Few-shot learning is a machine learning technique where a model learns to perform a new task from a small number of labeled examples, typically 1 to 10, instead of the thousands or millions of examples that traditional supervised learning requires. You show the model a handful of examples that demonstrate the pattern you want it to recognize, and it generalizes from those examples to handle new inputs it has never seen.

For document AI, few-shot learning answers a practical question that every team building document processing systems eventually faces: how do I get my model to handle a new document type without labeling thousands of pages first?

A team that has trained a model on invoices wants to add contracts. A hospital that has built an extraction pipeline for insurance claims needs to process a new form from a new insurer. A law firm that automated one type of filing wants to handle another. In each case, the traditional approach (label thousands of examples of the new type, fine-tune the model, evaluate, iterate) takes weeks to months. Few-shot learning offers a faster path: provide a small set of labeled examples and let the model adapt.

But few-shot learning for documents is harder than few-shot learning for plain text, and the realistic boundaries of what it can and cannot do are often obscured by vendor marketing. This guide covers how few-shot learning works, the three approaches that apply to documents, what tasks it handles well, where it breaks down, and how to decide between few-shot adaptation and full dataset labeling for your specific use case.

How Few-Shot Learning Works

Traditional supervised learning requires large labeled datasets because the model learns task-specific patterns from scratch. A text classifier trained on 50,000 labeled emails learns to distinguish spam from not-spam by seeing thousands of examples of each. Remove the training data and the model knows nothing.

Few-shot learning works differently. The model starts with broad knowledge acquired during pre-training (reading millions of documents, images, or text passages) and uses the few examples you provide to understand what specific task you want it to perform. The pre-training does the heavy lifting. The few-shot examples do the steering.

The terminology

Zero-shot: The model performs the task from instructions alone, with no examples. "Classify this document as an invoice, a contract, or a tax form." The model uses its pre-trained understanding of document types to make a prediction.

One-shot: The model sees one example of each category before performing the task. "Here is one invoice. Here is one contract. Here is one tax form. Now classify this new document."

Few-shot (typically 2 to 10 examples): The model sees a small set of examples per category. More examples produce more reliable generalization than one-shot, but the total example count remains small enough that labeling is fast.

Fine-tuning (hundreds to thousands of examples): The model's parameters are updated through gradient-based training on the labeled dataset. This is the traditional approach and produces the highest accuracy, but requires the most labeled data.

The boundaries between these categories are not rigid. In practice, the question is always: how many labeled examples do I need to reach acceptable accuracy on this specific task with this specific model?

Why Document Few-Shot Learning Is Harder Than Text

Few-shot learning on plain text (sentiment classification, topic categorization, entity extraction from sentences) has been well-studied and works reliably. Few-shot learning on documents is fundamentally harder because documents carry information in multiple modalities simultaneously.

Documents are visual, not just textual

A contract clause and a table cell might contain the same text ("10,000" is a contract value, a line-item total, a penalty amount, or a deposit. The visual context (where on the page, what surrounds it, what element type it sits inside) determines the meaning.

Few-shot learning for documents must capture both the text and the visual layout to generalize correctly. A model that learns from text-only examples misses the spatial patterns that distinguish identical text strings with different meanings.

Layout diversity within document types

Two invoices from different vendors may share the same fields (vendor name, invoice number, line items, total) but arrange them in completely different layouts. Few-shot learning must generalize across these layout variations, which means the few examples must cover enough layout diversity to represent the real distribution.

Three examples of invoices that all follow the same layout produce a model that works on that layout and fails on every other. Three examples that each use a different layout produce a model that generalizes better but may be less precise on any single layout.

Element types require structural understanding

Document AI tasks often involve distinguishing between element types (headers, paragraphs, tables, lists) that are defined by their visual structure rather than their text content. A header and a paragraph may contain similar text, but the header uses a larger font, sits at the top of a section, and is followed by body text. Few-shot models must learn these structural patterns from the examples, which requires either visual input (page images) or structural metadata (element type labels, bounding boxes).

Three Approaches to Few-Shot Document AI

Few-shot learning for documents takes three forms, each with different requirements, strengths, and limitations.

Approach 1: In-context prompting with vision-language models

The simplest approach. You provide a VLM (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini) with a document image and a few labeled examples directly in the prompt, then ask it to process a new document the same way.

Example prompt for invoice field extraction:

Here are two example invoices with their extracted fields:

Example 1:

[Image of invoice A]

Extracted fields:

- Vendor: Acme Corp

- Invoice Number: INV-2024-0042

- Total: $3,450.00

- Date: January 15, 2026

Example 2:

[Image of invoice B]

Extracted fields:

- Vendor: GlobalTech Ltd

- Invoice Number: GT-8891

- Total: $12,200.00

- Date: February 3, 2026

Now extract the same fields from this invoice:

[Image of new invoice]

Strengths: No training required. Works immediately. Handles new document types without any model updates. The VLM's massive pre-training provides the background knowledge. The examples provide the task specification.

Limitations: Accuracy is lower than fine-tuned models (typically 70 to 85% on field extraction versus 90%+ for fine-tuned models). Each inference call includes the examples in the prompt, consuming tokens and increasing cost. Performance varies significantly across VLM providers and versions. Complex documents with many fields or unusual layouts challenge in-context learning because the model must generalize from very few visual examples.

Best for: Rapid prototyping, low-volume document processing, testing whether a task is feasible before investing in labeled data.

Approach 2: Fine-tuning with few examples

This approach updates a pre-trained document model's parameters using a small labeled dataset. Models like LayoutLMv3, Donut, and Pix2Struct were pre-trained on millions of document pages, which means they already understand document structure at a general level. Fine-tuning adapts that general understanding to your specific task using your labeled examples.

The key insight: the pre-training did most of the work. A LayoutLMv3 model pre-trained on 11 million document images has already learned what headers look like, how tables are structured, and where fields typically appear. Fine-tuning on 50 to 200 labeled pages of your specific document type teaches the model the particular extraction rules your task requires.

Strengths: Higher accuracy than in-context prompting (typically 85 to 95% with 100 to 200 labeled pages). The model runs without prompt overhead after fine-tuning. Consistent, reproducible results. Works offline without API calls.

Limitations: Requires labeled training data (not zero-effort, even if the volume is small). Requires GPU compute for fine-tuning. The model must be retrained when new document types are added. Quality of the labeled examples matters enormously because the model has so few examples to learn from.

Best for: Production systems processing specific document types where accuracy above 85% is required.

Approach 3: Meta-learning for documents

Meta-learning ("learning to learn") trains a model to adapt quickly to new tasks by training it across many different tasks during a meta-training phase. Prototypical Networks, MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning), and Matching Networks are the primary meta-learning architectures applied to documents.

In the document context, meta-learning trains on many document classification or extraction tasks (invoices from 50 vendors, contracts from 30 firms, forms from 20 agencies), learning a representation space where documents of the same type cluster together. At inference time, the model receives a few examples of a new document type it has never seen, embeds them in this learned space, and classifies new documents by finding the nearest cluster.

Strengths: The fastest adaptation to truly novel document types (seconds, not hours). Works well for document classification (is this an invoice, a contract, or a tax form?). Requires no gradient updates at inference time.

Limitations: Lower accuracy on field-level extraction than fine-tuning. Requires a large meta-training corpus of diverse document tasks, which is expensive to create. Less widely supported by mainstream ML frameworks than fine-tuning approaches.

Best for: Systems that encounter frequently changing document types (mailroom automation, document routing, triage systems).

Five Document Tasks and How Many Examples Each Needs

The number of examples required for few-shot learning varies by task complexity. Document classification is simpler than field extraction, which is simpler than full layout detection. Here are realistic example counts based on published benchmarks and production systems.

Document classification

Task: Assign a category to an entire document (invoice, contract, medical record, tax form, letter).

Examples needed: 3 to 5 per class for in-context prompting. 10 to 30 per class for fine-tuning. Classification is the easiest few-shot task because the model needs to learn the overall visual and textual pattern of each document type, not the position of specific fields.

Why it works with few examples: Pre-trained models already understand that invoices contain line items and totals, contracts contain clauses and signature blocks, and tax forms contain numbered fields. The few-shot examples confirm which visual pattern maps to which label.

Document routing

Task: Route an incoming document to the correct department or workflow (claims to the claims team, contracts to legal, invoices to accounts payable).

Examples needed: 3 to 10 per routing destination. Routing is essentially classification with business-specific categories, so the example requirements are similar.

Named entity extraction

Task: Identify and extract specific entities (names, dates, amounts, addresses) from documents.

Examples needed: 10 to 30 per entity type for in-context prompting. 50 to 200 pages for fine-tuning. Named entity recognition on documents is harder than on plain text because the same entity type (a date, an amount) appears in different visual contexts across documents. The model needs enough examples to learn the visual and spatial patterns that indicate each entity type.

Field extraction (key-value pairs)

Task: Extract specific fields from structured documents (invoice total, contract effective date, patient name, policy number).

Examples needed: 15 to 50 pages for in-context prompting. 100 to 500 pages for fine-tuning to reach production accuracy (90%+). Field extraction is the task where few-shot learning most often falls short of production requirements, because each field has a specific spatial relationship to surrounding content that varies across document formats.

Layout detection

Task: Identify and classify every region on a document page (headers, paragraphs, tables, figures, lists, footers).

Examples needed: 50 to 200 pages for fine-tuning. Layout detection is the hardest few-shot task because the model must learn to detect and classify many element types simultaneously across diverse layouts. In-context prompting is generally not practical for layout detection because the output (bounding boxes with classifications for every region) is too structured for prompt-based generation.

For teams building layout detection training data, automated labeling tools can generate the initial labels for these 50 to 200 pages in minutes rather than the hours that manual annotation would require, making the "few" in few-shot fine-tuning achievable on a practical timeline.

When Few-Shot Is Not Enough

Few-shot learning is not a universal solution. It has clear boundaries, and understanding them prevents teams from investing in approaches that will not reach their accuracy requirements.

Complex extraction from diverse layouts

When the same field (invoice total, contract value, claim amount) appears in dozens of different positions across documents from different sources, few-shot examples cannot cover enough layout variation to generalize reliably. A model that has seen 10 invoice layouts will handle invoices from those 10 vendors but may fail on vendor 11.

The threshold varies by task, but as a rule: if your production documents come from more than 20 distinct layout templates, few-shot learning alone is unlikely to reach 90%+ extraction accuracy. You need a larger labeled dataset that covers the layout diversity.

High-accuracy requirements in regulated industries

Healthcare, financial services, and legal applications often require 95%+ field extraction accuracy because errors have compliance or financial consequences. Few-shot learning typically achieves 70 to 90% accuracy depending on the task and model. Closing the gap from 90% to 95%+ requires more labeled training data, not more clever prompting.

Multi-page document understanding

Few-shot examples work well for single-page tasks (classifying a page, extracting fields from a one-page form). Multi-page documents (contracts, reports, filings) require understanding relationships across pages (a term defined on page 3 is referenced on page 17), which few-shot learning has limited ability to demonstrate with 5 to 10 examples.

The honest decision framework

If few-shot accuracy meets your requirements, use it. It is faster and cheaper than building a full labeled dataset.

If few-shot accuracy is close but not sufficient (within 5 to 10 percentage points of your target), invest in labeled training data. The gap is almost always closed by more and better training data, not by changing the model or the prompting strategy.

If few-shot accuracy is far below your requirements (more than 15 percentage points below target), the task may be too complex for few-shot learning on your document types. You need a full training dataset with hundreds to thousands of labeled pages.

How Label Quality Affects Few-Shot Performance

When you have only 10 labeled examples, every example matters. A single incorrectly labeled example represents 10% of the training signal. In traditional supervised learning with 10,000 examples, one error is noise. In few-shot learning, one error is significant.

This means the quality of few-shot examples must be higher than the quality of examples in a large training dataset. Bounding boxes must be precise. Labels must be consistent. Taxonomy must be unambiguous. Edge cases must be excluded from the few-shot set (save them for evaluation, where you want to test generalization, not for training, where you want to teach the pattern).

The practical implication: spending 30 minutes carefully reviewing and correcting 10 few-shot examples produces better results than spending the same 30 minutes labeling 30 additional examples quickly. Quality beats quantity when quantity is necessarily small.

For teams preparing few-shot examples for document tasks, choosing the right taxonomy is particularly critical because taxonomy ambiguity, which a large dataset can overcome through sheer volume, derails a small dataset entirely. If "header" and "subheader" are not clearly distinguished in your taxonomy, the few examples you provide cannot teach the model the distinction.

Few-Shot Learning Combined With Other Approaches

In production systems, few-shot learning is rarely used in isolation. It is combined with other techniques to achieve accuracy that few-shot alone cannot reach.

Few-shot + RAG

Use few-shot examples to teach the model the output format and extraction pattern. Use RAG to supply the factual context (retrieved from a document database) that the model needs to answer specific questions. The few-shot examples handle the "how to respond" part. RAG handles the "what to respond with" part.

Example: A document QA system uses few-shot examples to teach the model to answer questions about contracts in a specific JSON format. RAG retrieves the relevant contract chunks. The few-shot format and the RAG content combine to produce a structured, accurate answer.

Few-shot + active learning

Start with a few-shot model that achieves 80% accuracy. Deploy it on production documents. Use the confidence scores to identify predictions the model is least certain about. Send those uncertain predictions to human reviewers for correction. Add the corrected examples to the training set. Retrain. The model improves with each cycle, and the human review effort is focused on the examples where human judgment adds the most value.

This active learning loop is the bridge between few-shot learning and full supervised learning. You start with few examples and systematically expand the dataset over time, guided by the model's own uncertainty.

Few-shot + pre-labeled data

Use automated labeling to generate initial labels for a larger set of documents. Review and correct a subset of those labels to create the few-shot training set. Use the remaining auto-labeled documents (with their confidence scores) as additional training signal, weighting high-confidence auto-labels more heavily than low-confidence ones.

This approach combines the speed of auto-labeling with the quality of human-reviewed few-shot examples, producing a training set that is both large enough for reliable generalization and high-quality enough for accurate learning.

Summary

Few-shot learning allows document AI models to adapt to new document types from a small number of labeled examples, typically 3 to 200 depending on the task. It works because pre-trained models (LayoutLMv3, Donut, GPT-4o, Claude) have already learned general document understanding from millions of documents during pre-training. The few-shot examples teach the model what specific task to perform, not how documents work in general.

Three approaches apply to documents: in-context prompting (fastest, lowest accuracy), fine-tuning with few examples (balanced, requires compute), and meta-learning (fastest adaptation, requires large meta-training corpus). Different document tasks need different example counts: classification needs 3 to 5, entity extraction needs 10 to 30, field extraction needs 15 to 50, and layout detection needs 50 to 200.

Few-shot learning has clear boundaries. Complex extraction across diverse layouts, high-accuracy requirements in regulated industries, and multi-page document understanding all exceed what few-shot approaches can reliably handle. When few-shot accuracy is insufficient, the path forward is more and better labeled training data, not more clever prompting.

The quality of few-shot examples matters more than the quantity. Ten carefully labeled, precisely annotated pages produce better few-shot results than 30 pages labeled quickly with inconsistent boundaries and ambiguous categories. For document AI, where labels include bounding boxes, element classifications, and spatial coordinates, this quality requirement makes the labeling tool and review process as important as the model architecture.
