9 Common Mistakes Teams Make When Labeling Training Data

Labeling training data is the single most important step in any machine learning pipeline. Not model selection. Not hyperparameter tuning. Not deployment architecture. The quality of your labels determines the ceiling of your model's performance, and no amount of downstream engineering can fix what bad labels break upstream.

Yet most teams treat labeling as a checkbox activity. Get the labels done, move on to training, and troubleshoot accuracy problems later. The result is weeks of model debugging that traces back to preventable errors in how the training data was prepared in the first place.

These are the nine mistakes we see most often, drawn from real-world document AI projects where label quality directly determined whether models shipped or stalled. Each one is avoidable, and the cost of avoiding it is always lower than the cost of discovering it after training.

1. Designing the Label Taxonomy Without Thinking About What the Model Needs to Learn

The most consequential labeling decision happens before a single document is annotated. The label taxonomy, the set of categories you will use to classify data, determines what your model can detect and what it will be blind to.

The mistake comes in two forms, and both are common.

Too few categories. A taxonomy that labels every text region as "text" gives the model no ability to distinguish between a page header, a section title, a body paragraph, a caption, and a footnote. The model learns that all text looks the same, which is true at the character level but useless at the document understanding level. When the application later needs to extract section headers separately from body text, the model cannot do it because the training data never taught it the difference.

Too many categories. A taxonomy with 40 granular categories (sub_header_level_3, bulleted_list_item_indented, table_caption_centered) creates classification boundaries that are ambiguous even for human annotators. When annotators cannot consistently agree on whether something is a "sub_header_level_2" or a "sub_header_level_3," the model learns noisy, unreliable patterns from the disagreement. The result is lower accuracy across all categories, not just the ambiguous ones.

The right approach is to start with a focused taxonomy that captures the structural distinctions your application actually requires, then expand only when the model's downstream task demands it. Our guide to choosing a document labeling taxonomy walks through this decision in detail.

2. Inconsistent Labels Across Annotators

When multiple people label the same type of data, they will interpret ambiguous cases differently unless the project provides explicit rules for every edge case. This is not a failure of the annotators. It is a failure of the annotation guidelines.

Consider a document page where a bold line of text sits between two paragraphs. Is it a section header or an emphasized sentence? Without a clear rule ("bold text on its own line with whitespace above and below is always classified as section_header"), Annotator A will call it a header and Annotator B will call it body text. The model trains on both labels for visually identical elements and learns an unstable pattern that it cannot apply reliably.

The damage from inconsistent labels is subtle because it does not show up as obviously wrong predictions. Instead, it shows up as a model that is confident on easy cases and unreliable on anything near a classification boundary. The boundary cases are exactly the ones that matter most in production.

How to prevent it: Write annotation guidelines that address the ten most common ambiguous cases your annotators will encounter. Include visual examples showing the correct classification for each one. Run a calibration round where all annotators label the same 50 pages independently, then compare results and discuss disagreements before the main labeling project begins.

3. Labeling Only One Document Type and Expecting the Model to Generalize

This mistake is particularly common in document AI projects. A team labels 5,000 pages of invoices from a single vendor, trains a layout detection model, and then deploys it on invoices from 200 different vendors. The model performs well on invoices that look like the training data and fails on everything else.

The problem is distribution mismatch. The model learned the visual patterns of one specific invoice layout, not the general concept of "what an invoice looks like." When it encounters a different font, a different column arrangement, or a different table structure, it has no training signal to draw from.

Research on the DocLayNet dataset demonstrated this directly: models trained exclusively on scientific paper layouts (PubLayNet, DocBank) showed significant accuracy drops when tested on financial reports, government documents, and technical manuals. The layout diversity of the training data mattered more than the volume.

If your model will encounter diverse document layouts in production, your training data must represent that diversity. Label documents from multiple sources, multiple formats, and multiple layout conventions. A model trained on 2,000 diverse pages will outperform a model trained on 10,000 pages from a single source.

4. Sloppy Bounding Boxes That Clip Content or Include Too Much Whitespace

In document data annotation, the bounding box is not a rough approximation. It is the ground truth that the model learns to replicate. If your bounding boxes consistently cut off the last line of a paragraph or include 50 pixels of whitespace above a header, the model will learn to reproduce those errors.

Clipped bounding boxes teach the model that elements are smaller than they actually are. A table bounding box that cuts off the bottom row means the model will learn to detect tables without their last row. In production, this causes extraction failures where the final line items in a table are missed.

Oversized bounding boxes teach the model that elements include surrounding whitespace or adjacent elements. A paragraph bounding box that extends into the next section means the model will learn blurry boundaries between elements. In production, this causes overlapping detections where one element's bounding box encroaches on another.

How to prevent it: Bounding boxes should tightly enclose the visual extent of each element with consistent, minimal padding. If using automated labeling, review a sample of the generated boxes in a visual editor that overlays them on the original document. A tool that displays color-coded bounding boxes over the source PDF makes spatial errors immediately visible.

5. Ignoring Tables or Treating Them as Plain Text

Tables are the single hardest element type in document labeling, and the most common response to that difficulty is to avoid dealing with them properly.

Some teams label tables as a single "text" region, flattening the row-column structure into a text block. Others draw a bounding box around the table but do not label internal structure (rows, columns, headers, cells). Still others skip tables entirely when they are too complex, leaving gaps in the training data that the model fills with guesses.

All three approaches produce models that fail on table extraction in production. And table extraction is frequently the most valuable capability in a document AI pipeline because tables contain the structured data (financial figures, line items, comparison metrics) that downstream systems need most.

If your application needs to extract tables from PDFs, your training data must include accurately labeled tables with correct boundaries. This means tight bounding boxes around the full table region, correct classification of the table as a distinct element type, and ideally some representation of internal structure (header rows versus data rows).

Tables with merged cells, invisible borders, or nested sub-tables are even harder, and omitting these edge cases from training data guarantees that the model will fail on them in production.

6. Labeling Everything Manually When Automation Covers 90% of the Work

The most expensive labeling mistake is not a wrong label. It is spending hours drawing bounding boxes by hand on work that a pre-trained model can do in seconds.

Manual annotation made sense in 2020 when automated labeling tools were immature. It no longer makes sense in 2026 for the structural layout labeling that accounts for the bulk of document AI training data preparation. Deep learning segmentation models, trained on large document corpora like PubLayNet and DocBank, can now identify and classify document regions (headers, paragraphs, tables, figures, lists, footers) with 90% or higher accuracy across a wide range of document types.

The most effective workflow is not fully manual or fully automated. It is a hybrid approach where an automated tool generates initial labels, and a human reviewer corrects the 5 to 10% of predictions that need adjustment. This produces the same label quality as fully manual annotation at a fraction of the time and cost.

Teams that resist automation often cite quality concerns. But the quality argument works in both directions. A human annotator working through their 400th page of bounding box annotation at the end of a long day will make more errors from fatigue than an automated model makes from statistical uncertainty. The difference is that the automated model flags its uncertain predictions with confidence scores. The fatigued annotator does not.

7. Not Including Edge Cases in the Training Data

Most labeled datasets are built from the cleanest, most representative examples available. This makes sense intuitively, but it creates a blind spot that surfaces painfully in production.

Real-world documents are messy. They include scanned pages with skewed text and low resolution. Pages with handwritten annotations over printed content. Multi-column layouts where reading order is ambiguous. Documents with watermarks, stamps, or background images that overlap with text regions. Pages where headers and footers vary from page to page within the same document.

If your training data consists entirely of clean, well-formatted, digital-native PDFs, the model has no signal for handling any of these variations. The first time it encounters a scanned document with a coffee stain, it will either misclassify the stained region or fail to detect elements entirely.

How to prevent it: Deliberately include degraded, noisy, and atypical documents in your training data. Label some scanned documents alongside digital-native ones. Include pages with handwritten markup. Include documents with unusual layouts. The goal is not to make these edge cases the majority of your dataset, but to ensure they are represented so the model has at least some training signal for handling them.

8. Evaluating Label Quality on the Same Distribution as the Training Data

A labeled dataset can look perfect when you evaluate it against itself and fail completely on documents from production.

This happens when the evaluation set is drawn from the same narrow distribution as the training data. If you labeled 5,000 pages of invoices from three vendors and hold out 500 of those pages for evaluation, the model's performance on that held-out set reflects how well it learned those three vendors' layouts. It says nothing about how the model will perform on invoices from the other 197 vendors it will encounter in production.

The fix is to evaluate on data that looks like production, not data that looks like training. Hold out documents from sources, formats, and conditions that the training set does not fully represent. If your training set is all digital-native PDFs, include scanned documents in your evaluation set. If your training set comes from five document sources, include documents from sources six through ten in your evaluation set.

A model that scores 95% on a training-like evaluation set and 78% on a production-like evaluation set is telling you something important about your training data's coverage gaps. Listening to that signal before deployment saves weeks of post-deployment debugging.

9. Labeling Once and Never Revisiting the Data

Many teams treat labeling as a one-time project. Build the dataset, train the model, ship it. If accuracy is good enough, move on. If accuracy is not good enough, add more data and retrain.

This "label once" approach misses the most powerful feedback loop in ML development: using model performance to identify and fix label errors.

After a model is trained, its prediction errors are not random. They cluster around specific document patterns where the training labels are ambiguous, inconsistent, or wrong. A model that consistently misclassifies a certain type of text block is often revealing a labeling error that propagated through the training data. Fixing that labeling error and retraining produces a bigger accuracy improvement than adding 1,000 more labeled pages that repeat the same mistake.

This is the core principle behind data-centric AI: iterating on data quality produces better models than iterating on model architecture. The teams that build the best document AI systems are not the ones with the largest training sets. They are the ones that treat labeling as an ongoing process where model outputs inform label corrections, label corrections improve the model, and the cycle repeats.

The benefits of automated labeling become especially clear in this iterative workflow. When re-labeling a batch of documents takes seconds instead of hours, the feedback loop between model training and data correction accelerates from weeks to days. Each iteration catches errors that the previous iteration missed, and the model improves faster with each cycle than it would from simply adding more data.

How These Mistakes Compound

These nine mistakes do not operate independently. They interact and amplify each other.

A poorly designed taxonomy (Mistake 1) makes inconsistent labeling (Mistake 2) worse because annotators are trying to apply categories that do not map cleanly to the data. Narrow training data (Mistake 3) combined with training-like evaluation (Mistake 8) masks the distribution problem until production deployment reveals it. Manual-only labeling (Mistake 6) makes iterative label correction (Mistake 9) impractical because each iteration requires another round of expensive, time-consuming human work.

The compounding effect means that fixing any single mistake produces outsized improvements. Get the taxonomy right and inconsistency drops. Automate the base labeling and iteration becomes fast. Include edge cases and production accuracy improves. Evaluate on production-like data and you catch distribution gaps before deployment.

A Practical Checklist Before Your Next Labeling Project

Before starting or restarting a training data labeling effort, run through these questions:

Does my taxonomy match the distinctions my model actually needs to make? Have I looked at our taxonomy design guide and compared my categories against what our application requires?

Have I written annotation guidelines with visual examples for every ambiguous case?

Does my training data represent the full range of document types, sources, and conditions my model will encounter in production?

Am I using automated labeling for the structural work and reserving human effort for corrections and edge cases?

Does my evaluation set look like production data, not like training data?

Have I planned for at least two label correction cycles after initial model training?

Every "no" answer identifies a specific risk to model performance. Addressing them before labeling begins costs hours. Addressing them after deployment costs weeks.

Summary

The nine most common labeling mistakes are designing the wrong taxonomy, inconsistent labels across annotators, training on narrow document distributions, sloppy bounding boxes, ignoring tables, labeling everything manually, excluding edge cases, evaluating on training-like data, and treating labeling as a one-time project.

What connects all nine is a pattern: treating labeling as a mechanical task to finish rather than an engineering decision that shapes every aspect of model performance. The teams that avoid these mistakes approach labeling with the same rigor they bring to model architecture and evaluation design. They choose their annotation types deliberately, design their taxonomy based on the model's task, validate on production-like data, and iterate on their labels as aggressively as they iterate on their code.

The result is not just better labels. It is better models, shipped faster, with fewer surprises in production.
