How to Choose the Right Document Labeling Taxonomy

A document labeling taxonomy is the structured set of label categories used to classify and annotate the regions, entities, and relationships within a document. It defines what your AI model will learn to recognize: which parts of a page are headers, which are tables, which are footnotes, and how those elements relate to each other.

Choosing the wrong taxonomy is one of the most expensive mistakes in document AI. If your labels are too coarse, the model cannot distinguish between elements that matter for your use case. If they are too granular, annotation becomes slow, inter-annotator agreement drops, and the model struggles to learn rare categories. If the labels overlap, annotators make inconsistent choices that inject noise into the training data.

This guide walks through how to evaluate, select, and customize a document labeling taxonomy, whether you are building a document AI system from scratch, fine-tuning a pre-trained model, or choosing an automated tool. What Is a Document Labeling Taxonomy?

A taxonomy, in the context of document labeling, is a predefined list of categories (labels) that annotators or automated systems assign to regions within a document. Each label represents a distinct structural or semantic element.

At the layout level, a taxonomy might include labels like "Title," "Section Header," "Paragraph," "Table," "Figure," and "Footnote." At the semantic level, it might include labels like "Party Name," "Contract Date," "Indemnification Clause," or "Revenue Line Item."

The taxonomy serves three functions simultaneously. First, it is the instruction set for human annotators: it tells them exactly what to look for and how to categorize what they find. Second, it defines the output space of the machine learning model: the model can only predict categories that exist in the taxonomy. Third, it determines the downstream utility of the annotated data: if the taxonomy does not include a label for "table header" versus "table data cell," no amount of model training will teach the system to distinguish between them.

A well-designed taxonomy makes annotation faster, improves inter-annotator agreement, produces cleaner training data, and results in higher model accuracy. A poorly designed taxonomy does the opposite at every step.

The Three Standard Document Layout Taxonomies

Before designing a custom taxonomy, it helps to understand the three public taxonomies that define the current state of document layout analysis research. Each represents a different trade-off between simplicity and detail.

PubLayNet: 5 Labels

PubLayNet, created by IBM Research, is the largest automatically generated document layout dataset. It contains over 360,000 document images sourced from PubMed Central scientific articles. Its taxonomy uses five labels:

  • Text (paragraphs and body content)
  • Title (document and section titles)
  • List (bulleted and numbered lists)
  • Table (tabular content)
  • Figure (images, charts, diagrams)

PubLayNet's strength is its simplicity. Five labels are easy for annotators to apply consistently, and models trained on this taxonomy achieve high accuracy because the categories are visually distinct and rarely ambiguous. Its limitation is that it was built exclusively from scientific articles, so it handles research paper layouts well but struggles with the diverse layouts found in financial reports, legal contracts, or government filings.

Best for: Teams that need a simple, proven baseline for scientific or technical document processing and do not require fine-grained structural detail.

DocBank: 13 Labels

DocBank, developed by Microsoft Research and Harbin Institute of Technology, contains 500,000 document pages from arXiv papers. It uses weak supervision (aligning LaTeX source code to PDF output) to generate token-level annotations with 13 labels:

Abstract, Author, Caption, Date, Equation, Figure, Footer, List, Paragraph, Reference, Section, Table, Title.

DocBank's advantage is its token-level granularity: each word in the document is labeled, not just bounding-box regions. This enables training for models that need to understand text content alongside visual layout. Its limitation is the same domain constraint as PubLayNet: it draws exclusively from academic papers on arXiv.

Best for: Teams building models that need both layout and textual understanding of scientific documents, especially for fine-tuning vision-language models like LayoutLM.

DocLayNet: 11 Labels

DocLayNet, also from IBM Research, is the most diverse and carefully annotated public dataset. It contains 80,863 manually annotated pages from six document categories: Financial Reports, Manuals, Scientific Articles, Laws and Regulations, Patents, and Government Tenders. Its 11 labels are:

  • Caption (text introducing a picture or table)
  • Footnote (notes at the bottom of a page)
  • Formula (mathematical or chemical expressions)
  • List-item (individual items in a bulleted or numbered list)
  • Page-footer (repeating footer content like page numbers)
  • Page-header (repeating header content like document titles)
  • Picture (graphics, photographs, charts)
  • Section-header (headings and subheadings)
  • Table (tabular data)
  • Text (regular paragraphs and body content)
  • Title (the overall document title)

DocLayNet's key advantage is layout variability. Because it includes financial reports with company-specific artistic layouts, dense legal documents, and complex patent filings, models trained on DocLayNet generalize far better to real-world enterprise documents than models trained on PubLayNet or DocBank alone. Research has shown that DocLayNet-trained models are the preferred choice for general-purpose document layout analysis.

Best for: Teams building document AI systems that need to handle diverse, real-world document types across industries. This is the closest starting point for most enterprise use cases.

Five Principles for Designing a Document Labeling Taxonomy

Whether you adopt a standard taxonomy or design a custom one, five principles determine whether it will work well in practice. These principles, drawn from recent large-scale taxonomy research, apply equally to document layout labels, entity labels, and semantic labels.

1. Orthogonality: Each Label Should Capture Independent Information

Every label in your taxonomy should represent a distinct category that does not overlap with other labels. If annotators frequently hesitate between two labels for the same region, those labels are not orthogonal, and you either need to merge them, split them differently, or add clearer guidelines.

For example, "Section Header" and "Title" are orthogonal in DocLayNet because a document has one title but many section headers, and they appear in different positions with different visual styling. But if you added a "Subtitle" label without clear rules about what distinguishes it from "Section Header," annotators would produce inconsistent annotations.

Test: For any pair of labels in your taxonomy, ask: "Can an annotator reliably distinguish between these two for any document region they encounter?" If the answer is no, the taxonomy needs revision.

2. Expressivity: The Taxonomy Must Be Detailed Enough for Your Use Case

The taxonomy must capture the distinctions that matter for your downstream application. A taxonomy that works for general document layout analysis may not work for a specific extraction task.

If your goal is to extract table data from financial statements, you need labels that distinguish "Table Header Row," "Table Data Row," "Table Caption," and "Table Footnote." A single "Table" label is insufficient because it treats the entire table as one undifferentiated block, and your extraction model cannot learn the internal structure. For a deeper look at this specific challenge, see our guide on extracting tables from PDFs with machine learning.

If your goal is to build a document search system, you may only need "Title," "Section Header," and "Body Text" because those are the elements the retrieval system uses for chunking and indexing.

Test: For each downstream task your model needs to perform, ask: "Does the taxonomy contain every label distinction the model needs to make this decision?" If you can imagine a scenario where the model needs to treat two differently labeled regions the same way, you may have too many labels. If you can imagine a scenario where the model needs to treat two identically labeled regions differently, you do not have enough.

3. Correctness: Labels Must Be Consistently Assignable

A taxonomy is correct when trained annotators consistently agree on which label to assign to a given document region. This is measured empirically through inter-annotator agreement (IAA).

DocLayNet's research team measured this directly. They had multiple annotators independently label the same pages and found that even well-trained human experts fall about 10% short of perfect agreement on 11-class document layout annotation. That sets a practical ceiling: your model cannot outperform the consistency of its training labels.

If your taxonomy has 20+ labels and your annotators achieve a Cohen's Kappa below 0.7, the taxonomy is too complex for reliable annotation. Either reduce the number of labels, merge ambiguous categories, or invest more in annotator training and guideline clarity.

Test: Run a pilot annotation on 200 to 500 documents with at least two independent annotators. Measure Cohen's Kappa or Fleiss' Kappa. If agreement is below 0.7, revise the taxonomy before scaling annotation.

4. Efficiency: The Taxonomy Must Be Practical to Apply

A taxonomy that is theoretically perfect but takes annotators five minutes per page is impractical for large-scale annotation. Efficiency means that the typical document region can be classified quickly and confidently by a trained annotator.

Flat taxonomies (a single list of labels with no hierarchy) are faster to apply than hierarchical taxonomies (labels organized in parent-child trees). However, flat taxonomies become unwieldy beyond about 15 to 20 labels because annotators must scan a long list for each decision. Hierarchical taxonomies scale better because annotators make a series of narrowing decisions (first "Is this a table or text?", then "Is this a table header or table data?").

Test: Time your annotators on a representative sample. If average annotation time per page exceeds your budget constraints, simplify the taxonomy or invest in automated pre-annotation to reduce manual work.

5. Effectiveness: The Taxonomy Must Improve Downstream Performance

A taxonomy matters only if it produces training data that makes your model perform better on its intended task. This is the ultimate test, and it is empirical.

If adding a new label to the taxonomy does not improve the model's accuracy, precision, or recall on the task that label was meant to support, the label is adding annotation cost without adding value. Remove it.

If merging two labels into one does not hurt model performance, those labels were not carrying useful information separately. Keep them merged.

Test: Train your model with and without the label in question. Compare performance metrics on a held-out validation set. Keep only the labels that measurably improve performance.

Domain-Specific Taxonomy Guidance

Standard layout taxonomies cover structural elements (headers, tables, paragraphs). But domain-specific document AI systems also need semantic labels that capture the meaning of content within those structures. Here is guidance for the four most common enterprise document domains.

Legal Documents

Legal documents (contracts, agreements, regulatory filings, court documents) require a taxonomy that captures:

Structural labels: Title, Section Header, Paragraph, Table, Signature Block, Exhibit/Attachment Header, Recitals Section, Definitions Section.

Semantic entity labels: Party Name, Effective Date, Jurisdiction, Governing Law, Monetary Amount, Duration/Term, Defined Term.

Clause classification labels: Indemnification, Limitation of Liability, Force Majeure, Termination, Confidentiality, Non-Compete, Assignment, Dispute Resolution, Representations and Warranties.

The key challenge in legal taxonomy design is that clause boundaries are not always visually distinct. A single paragraph might contain both a representation and a warranty. Your taxonomy needs rules for how to handle multi-label regions.

Financial Documents

Financial documents (annual reports, SEC filings, audit reports, invoices, bank statements) require:

Structural labels: Title, Section Header, Paragraph, Table, Chart/Graph, Footnote, Disclaimer, Page Header, Page Footer.

Semantic entity labels: Company Name, Reporting Period, Currency, Account Name, Line Item Amount, Auditor Name, Filing Date.

Table-specific labels: Table Header Row, Table Sub-Header, Table Data Cell, Table Total Row, Table Footnote Reference. These distinctions are critical because financial tables contain hierarchical information that a single "Table" label cannot capture. Our guide on PDF data extraction methods covers the technical approaches for handling this complexity.

Medical and Healthcare Documents

Medical documents (clinical reports, lab results, discharge summaries, insurance claims) require:

Structural labels: Title, Section Header, Paragraph, Table, Image/Scan, Patient Information Block, Physician Notes Section.

Semantic entity labels: Patient Identifier, Date of Service, Diagnosis Code, Procedure Code, Medication Name, Dosage, Provider Name, Facility Name.

Section classification labels: Chief Complaint, History of Present Illness, Assessment, Plan, Lab Results, Imaging Results, Medication List.

Medical taxonomy design must account for privacy constraints. Labels that directly identify individuals (name, date of birth, medical record number) need to be handled with strict access controls throughout the annotation pipeline, consistent with HIPAA requirements.

Insurance Documents

Insurance documents (claims, policies, endorsements, loss reports) require:

Structural labels: Title, Section Header, Paragraph, Table, Form Field, Checkbox/Radio, Signature, Endorsement Header.

Semantic entity labels: Policy Number, Claim Number, Insured Name, Date of Loss, Loss Location, Coverage Type, Deductible Amount, Claim Amount.

Section classification labels: Declaration Page, Coverage Summary, Exclusions, Conditions, Endorsements, Loss Description, Adjuster Notes.

Flat vs. Hierarchical Taxonomies

Taxonomy structure affects annotation speed, annotator consistency, and model architecture.

Flat Taxonomy

A flat taxonomy is a single-level list of labels with no parent-child relationships. DocLayNet's 11 labels and PubLayNet's 5 labels are flat taxonomies.

Advantages: Simple to implement. Annotators make one decision per region. Works directly with standard object detection models (Faster R-CNN, YOLO, DETR) that predict a single class per bounding box. Fewer labels mean higher inter-annotator agreement.

Disadvantages: Does not capture relationships between labels. Cannot represent that "Table Header" and "Table Data" are both sub-types of "Table." Becomes unwieldy beyond 15 to 20 labels because annotators must scan a long undifferentiated list.

Best for: Layout analysis tasks, document segmentation, and any project where the downstream model uses a standard detection or segmentation architecture.

Hierarchical Taxonomy

A hierarchical taxonomy organizes labels in a tree structure. A top-level "Table" label might have children: "Table Header," "Table Body," "Table Footer," "Table Caption." A top-level "Text" label might have children: "Paragraph," "Block Quote," "Callout Box."

Advantages: Scales to large label sets without overwhelming annotators, because each annotation decision is a series of narrowing choices. Captures structural relationships that flat labels cannot. Enables multi-resolution analysis: a model can predict at the coarse level ("this is a Table") or the fine level ("this is a Table Header Row") depending on the task.

Disadvantages: More complex to implement in annotation tools and model architectures. Annotators must learn the full hierarchy. Multi-level predictions are harder to evaluate with standard metrics.

Best for: Complex extraction tasks (key-value extraction from forms, table structure recognition, legal clause classification) where the downstream application needs fine-grained distinctions within broader categories.

When to Use Each

Start flat. Add hierarchy only when a flat taxonomy cannot capture a distinction your downstream task requires. This approach minimizes annotation complexity while ensuring your taxonomy is as expressive as your use case demands.

If you are unsure, DocLayNet's 11-label flat taxonomy is the strongest starting point for general-purpose document layout analysis. Extend it with domain-specific semantic labels as needed.

Common Taxonomy Design Mistakes

Starting Too Granular

Teams often begin with 30+ labels because they want to capture every possible distinction. This produces low inter-annotator agreement, slow annotation speed, and a model that struggles to learn rare categories. Start with 5 to 11 structural labels. Add granularity only when evaluation shows the model needs finer distinctions.

Overlapping Label Definitions

If "Caption" and "Footnote" are not clearly differentiated in the guidelines, annotators will use them interchangeably. Every label must have a definition that includes: (1) what the label covers, (2) what it does not cover, (3) positive examples, (4) negative examples, and (5) edge-case rules.

Ignoring Label Frequency Distribution

If 95% of regions in your documents are "Text" and only 0.1% are "Formula," the model will learn to predict "Text" for everything and still achieve high overall accuracy. Rare labels need targeted strategies: oversampling in training data, class-weighted loss functions, or a two-stage detection approach. Before finalizing a taxonomy, analyze the expected label frequency distribution across your document corpus.

No Versioning Strategy

Taxonomies evolve. New document types require new labels. Old labels get renamed or merged. Without a versioning strategy, you cannot maintain consistency between training data labeled under version 1 and new data labeled under version 2. Every taxonomy should have a version number, a changelog, and mapping rules between versions.

Designing Without Annotator Input

The people who will apply the labels (annotators) know where the ambiguity lies. Designing a taxonomy in isolation and handing it to annotators without a feedback loop produces a taxonomy that works in theory but fails in practice. Run a pilot with real annotators on real documents before committing to any taxonomy.

Step-by-Step Framework for Choosing Your Taxonomy

Step 1: Define the downstream task. What will the model do with the labeled data? Document classification? Layout segmentation? Key-value extraction? Clause detection? Knowledge graph construction? The task determines which distinctions the taxonomy must capture.

Step 2: Audit your document corpus. Collect a representative sample of 100 to 500 documents. Identify the structural elements that appear frequently. Note elements that vary across document types or sources.

Step 3: Start with a standard taxonomy. Use DocLayNet's 11 labels as the baseline for layout annotation. Add domain-specific semantic labels only for the entities and relationships your downstream task requires.

Step 4: Write annotation guidelines. For each label, write a definition, positive examples, negative examples, and edge-case rules. Test the guidelines with two to three annotators on a pilot batch of 50 to 100 documents.

Step 5: Measure inter-annotator agreement. Calculate Cohen's Kappa or Fleiss' Kappa on the pilot batch. If agreement is below 0.7, revise the taxonomy and guidelines. Repeat until agreement exceeds 0.7.

Step 6: Evaluate downstream impact. Train a model on the pilot data and measure task-specific performance. Add or remove labels based on whether they improve the metrics that matter for your use case.

Step 7: Document and version. Record the final taxonomy with version number, label definitions, guideline document, and mapping rules. This documentation is not optional: the EU AI Act's Article 10 now requires auditable annotation process documentation for high-risk AI systems.

Step 8: Scale annotation. With a validated taxonomy and clear guidelines, scale annotation using manual teams, automated tools, or a hybrid approach. DocuGraph's auto-labeling can serve as the automated pre-annotation step, with human reviewers correcting edge cases flagged by low-confidence scores.

FAQ: Document Labeling Taxonomy Questions

What is a document labeling taxonomy? A document labeling taxonomy is the structured set of label categories used to classify regions, entities, and relationships within documents for machine learning training. It defines what the AI model will learn to recognize: headers, tables, paragraphs, entities, clause types, and more.

How many labels should a document taxonomy have? For layout analysis, 5 to 11 labels is the proven range. PubLayNet uses 5, DocLayNet uses 11. For domain-specific semantic annotation (legal clauses, financial fields), add 5 to 15 semantic labels on top of the layout labels. Keep the total under 25 to 30 unless you have a hierarchical structure and experienced annotators.

What is the best starting taxonomy for document AI? DocLayNet's 11-label taxonomy is the strongest general-purpose starting point. It covers Caption, Footnote, Formula, List-item, Page-footer, Page-header, Picture, Section-header, Table, Text, and Title. Trained on diverse real-world documents from finance, law, science, patents, and government, it generalizes better than taxonomies built from academic papers alone.

Should I use a flat or hierarchical taxonomy? Start flat. Add hierarchy only when your downstream task requires fine-grained distinctions within broader categories (for example, distinguishing table header rows from table data rows). Flat taxonomies are simpler to implement, faster to annotate, and produce higher inter-annotator agreement.

How do I measure whether my taxonomy is working? Measure inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's Kappa above 0.7 minimum) to confirm the taxonomy is consistently assignable. Then measure downstream model performance (accuracy, precision, recall, F1) on a held-out validation set to confirm the taxonomy produces useful training data.
