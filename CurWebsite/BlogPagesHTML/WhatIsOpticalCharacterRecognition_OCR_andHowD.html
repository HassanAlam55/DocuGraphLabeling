What Is Optical Character Recognition (OCR) and How Does It Work?

Optical character recognition turns images of text into text that a computer can read, search, copy, and process. You scan a paper document or upload a PDF image, and OCR converts the pixels that look like letters into actual digital characters. Without OCR, a scanned invoice is just a picture. With OCR, it becomes searchable text with every word, number, and symbol accessible to software.

The technology has existed since the 1950s, but the version running in 2026 bears almost no resemblance to those early systems. Modern OCR uses neural networks trained on millions of text samples across hundreds of languages and font styles. It handles printed text at 98 to 99% character-level accuracy, reads handwriting with increasing reliability, and processes documents in over 100 languages.

But OCR also has clear limits. It tells you what characters are on a page. It does not tell you what those characters mean, what type of document they belong to, or how different text regions relate to each other structurally. Understanding where OCR ends and where more advanced document AI begins is essential for anyone building document processing systems in 2026.

This guide covers how OCR works at each stage of the pipeline, what output formats it produces, where it fails, and how it fits into the broader document understanding stack.

How OCR Works: The Six-Stage Pipeline

Every OCR system, from a free mobile scanning app to an enterprise document processing platform, follows the same fundamental pipeline. The stages run in sequence, and each one depends on the quality of the one before it.

Stage 1: Image acquisition

The pipeline starts with an image. This can be a photograph of a document taken with a phone camera, a page scanned through a flatbed or sheet-fed scanner, a faxed document stored as a TIFF file, or a page from a PDF that contains only image data (no embedded text layer).

The quality of this input image determines the ceiling for everything that follows. A 300 DPI scan of a clean printed page gives OCR the best possible input. A 72 DPI photograph of a crumpled receipt taken in poor lighting gives it the worst. No amount of algorithmic sophistication in later stages can fully compensate for a low-quality input image.

Stage 2: Preprocessing

Before the OCR engine attempts to read any characters, it processes the image to maximize readability. Preprocessing includes several operations, each addressing a specific quality issue.

Binarization converts the image from color or grayscale to pure black and white. Characters become black pixels on a white background. This simplification makes character boundaries easier for the recognition engine to detect. The most common binarization method, Otsu's algorithm, automatically selects the threshold that separates foreground (text) from background.

Deskewing detects and corrects page rotation. A page scanned at a 2-degree angle produces text lines that slope across the image. The deskewing algorithm detects the dominant text angle (typically using the Hough transform to find the most common line orientation) and rotates the image to align text horizontally.

Noise removal eliminates stray marks, scanner artifacts, and background texture that could be mistaken for characters. Median filtering removes salt-and-pepper noise. Morphological operations (erosion and dilation) clean up character edges. Adaptive thresholding handles documents with uneven lighting or stained backgrounds.

Resolution normalization scales the image to the resolution the recognition engine was trained on. Most modern OCR engines expect input at 200 to 300 DPI. An image at 72 DPI is upscaled (though upscaling adds no real detail). An image at 600 DPI is downscaled (reducing processing time without losing recognizable detail).

Border removal strips black borders, scanning artifacts, and punch hole shadows from the edges of the image. These non-text elements can confuse the layout analysis and character recognition stages.

Stage 3: Layout analysis

Before recognizing individual characters, the OCR engine needs to understand how the text is organized on the page. Layout analysis identifies text regions, determines reading order, and segments the page into processing blocks.

The engine identifies text blocks (rectangular regions containing text), text lines within each block, and individual words within each line. On a simple single-column document, this segmentation is straightforward: one block, lines from top to bottom, words from left to right.

On complex documents (multi-column layouts, documents with sidebars, pages with embedded tables and figures), layout analysis becomes the stage where most errors originate. A two-column page requires the engine to recognize the column boundary and process each column independently. If it fails to detect the boundary, it reads across both columns on each line, interleaving text from column one with text from column two.

This layout analysis stage is related to but more limited than the document layout analysis performed by document AI systems. OCR layout analysis identifies where text is. Document AI layout analysis identifies what type of content each region contains (header, paragraph, table, figure, footer) and how those regions relate to each other structurally.

Stage 4: Character recognition

This is the core of the OCR pipeline: identifying each character in the segmented text regions. Modern OCR engines use one or more recognition approaches.

Pattern matching compares the image of each character against a database of stored character templates (glyphs). When the scanned character matches a template closely enough, the engine assigns that template's identity to the character. Pattern matching works well on standard fonts that match the template database and fails on fonts, sizes, or styles it has not seen.

Feature extraction analyzes the geometric properties of each character: the number of horizontal and vertical strokes, the presence of curves, loops, intersections, and endpoints. These features are compared against feature profiles for known characters. Feature extraction generalizes better than pattern matching because it works on characters with unfamiliar fonts, as long as the geometric features are recognizable.

Neural network recognition uses deep learning models (typically convolutional neural networks or recurrent neural networks) trained on millions of character images to classify each character. The model learns to recognize characters from the statistical patterns in pixel data rather than from explicit rules or templates. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) networks are particularly effective for OCR because they process character sequences and use context from surrounding characters to improve accuracy. Modern OCR engines like Tesseract 5 use LSTM-based recognition as their primary method.

Transformer-based recognition is the newest approach, used in engines like TrOCR and PaddleOCR's latest models. Transformers process the entire text line as a sequence and use self-attention mechanisms to capture relationships between characters across the full line. This context awareness helps resolve ambiguous characters (is that mark a period or a comma?) based on the surrounding text.

Stage 5: Post-processing

The raw recognition output contains errors. Post-processing applies corrections using language context and statistical patterns.

Dictionary lookup checks recognized words against a language dictionary. If a recognized word does not match any dictionary entry, the engine suggests corrections based on similar words (replacing "tbe" with "the," for example). This works well for common words and fails on proper nouns, technical terms, and domain-specific vocabulary.

Language modeling uses statistical models of word sequences (n-gram models or neural language models) to evaluate whether the recognized text makes linguistic sense. If the engine recognizes "the cat sat on the mat," the language model confirms the sequence is plausible. If it recognizes "the cat sat on the mbt," the model flags "mbt" as unlikely and suggests corrections.

Format validation checks recognized text against expected patterns. If the engine is processing a field known to contain a date, it validates that the recognized text matches a date format (MM/DD/YYYY). If the field is known to contain a phone number, it validates the digit count and formatting. Format validation requires knowing what type of content each field contains, which is information that OCR alone typically does not have.

Confidence scoring assigns a probability value to each recognized character, word, or line. High confidence (0.95+) indicates the engine is certain about the recognition. Low confidence (below 0.80) indicates ambiguity that may benefit from human review. Confidence scores are critical for production systems because they enable automated routing: high-confidence text proceeds to downstream processing while low-confidence text is flagged for manual verification.

Stage 6: Output generation

The final stage converts the recognized text into a structured output format. OCR engines produce several formats depending on the use case.

Plain text is the simplest output: a string of recognized characters with line breaks. No positional information, no formatting, no confidence scores. Useful for full-text search indexing and basic text extraction.

hOCR (HTML-based OCR output) embeds recognized text in an HTML structure with bounding box coordinates for each word, line, and paragraph. The positional information makes hOCR useful for applications that need to know where each word appears on the page.

Example hOCR output for a single word:

<span class="ocrx_word" title="bbox 100 200 180 220;

  x_wconf 96">Invoice</span>

This tells you the word "Invoice" was recognized at pixel coordinates [100, 200, 180, 220] with 96% confidence.

ALTO XML (Analyzed Layout and Text Object) is an XML format commonly used in digital library and archival applications. It provides detailed structural information including text blocks, text lines, and individual character positions (called "strings" and "glyphs").

TSV (tab-separated values) output from Tesseract provides word-level data with positional coordinates, confidence scores, and hierarchical structure (page, block, paragraph, line, word). This format is commonly used for feeding OCR output into data analysis pipelines.

JSON output, supported by newer OCR APIs and engines, provides the same positional and confidence data in a format that integrates directly with web applications and ML pipelines.

The choice of output format depends on what happens after OCR. If the text feeds into a search index, plain text is sufficient. If it feeds into a document AI pipeline that needs positional coordinates alongside text, hOCR or JSON is necessary. If it feeds into a vision-language model like LayoutLMv3, the word-level bounding boxes from TSV or JSON output become the positional embeddings that the model requires alongside the text tokens.

Types of OCR

OCR is not a single technology. Several variants handle different recognition tasks.

Standard OCR

Recognizes printed text in standard fonts. This is what most people mean when they say "OCR." It handles machine-printed characters on clean backgrounds with high accuracy (98 to 99% character-level on modern engines).

Intelligent Character Recognition (ICR)

Extends OCR to handwritten text. ICR uses machine learning models trained on handwriting samples to recognize characters that vary in shape, size, slant, and spacing. Accuracy is lower than printed text OCR (typically 80 to 95% depending on handwriting legibility) because handwriting has far more variation than machine-printed text.

Optical Mark Recognition (OMR)

Detects marks rather than characters: filled bubbles on standardized tests, checked boxes on forms, signatures on documents. OMR does not recognize what the mark says. It recognizes that a mark exists at a specific location.

Optical Word Recognition (OWR)

Recognizes entire words as units rather than individual characters. OWR uses word-level dictionaries and can handle degraded text where individual characters are unrecognizable but the overall word shape is distinctive enough to match a dictionary entry.

Full-page OCR

Combines layout analysis with character recognition to process entire document pages, handling mixed content (text, tables, images, headers, footers) and maintaining reading order across complex layouts.

OCR Accuracy: What the Numbers Actually Mean

OCR accuracy is reported in two ways, and the distinction matters.

Character-level accuracy measures what percentage of individual characters are recognized correctly. Modern OCR engines achieve 98 to 99% character-level accuracy on clean printed text in standard fonts. That sounds impressive until you calculate what it means for a real document: a page with 2,000 characters at 99% accuracy contains 20 incorrectly recognized characters. On a 50-page document, that is 1,000 errors.

Word-level accuracy measures what percentage of complete words are recognized correctly. A single character error makes the entire word wrong. At 99% character accuracy, word-level accuracy on 5-character words drops to approximately 95% (because each character has a 1% chance of error, and any error ruins the word). Longer words have lower word-level accuracy at the same character-level accuracy.

Accuracy by document type

OCR accuracy varies dramatically by input quality:

Clean printed text, standard fonts, 300 DPI scan: 98 to 99% character accuracy. This is the benchmark that vendors cite in marketing materials.

Standard printed text, mixed fonts, 200 DPI scan: 95 to 98% character accuracy. Font mixing and lower resolution introduce more ambiguity.

Degraded scans (low contrast, staining, fading): 85 to 95% character accuracy. The preprocessing stage compensates for some degradation, but severely faded or stained text produces recognition errors that no amount of post-processing fixes.

Handwritten text (neat, consistent handwriting): 85 to 95% character accuracy with modern ICR engines.

Handwritten text (variable, casual handwriting): 60 to 85% character accuracy. Highly variable handwriting remains one of OCR's weakest areas.

Photographed documents (phone camera, variable lighting): 80 to 95% character accuracy. Camera angle distortion, uneven lighting, and motion blur all reduce accuracy compared to flatbed scans.

Mixed content (text with tables, figures, stamps): Variable. The text recognition accuracy may be high, but layout analysis errors cause reading order mistakes that produce garbled output even when individual characters are recognized correctly.

Where OCR Fails: Five Fundamental Limitations

Understanding OCR's limitations is as important as understanding its capabilities, especially for teams deciding whether OCR alone is sufficient or whether they need document AI capabilities on top of it.

1. OCR does not understand document structure

OCR extracts text. It does not classify that text by type. A page header, a body paragraph, a table cell, a footnote, and a page number are all just "text" in the OCR output. OCR cannot tell you which text is the invoice total versus a line-item amount, which text is a section heading versus body copy, or which text belongs to a table versus a paragraph.

This structural understanding requires document layout analysis and semantic segmentation, which are document AI capabilities that operate on top of OCR output.

2. OCR destroys table structure

OCR can recognize the text inside table cells. It cannot preserve the row-column relationships between cells. A table with headers "Product," "Quantity," and "Price" comes out as a flat text stream where the association between values and headers is lost. Extracting table structure from PDFs requires specialized table detection models that understand grid layouts, not just character recognition.

3. OCR produces unreliable reading order on complex layouts

Single-column documents are read top to bottom, left to right. OCR handles this correctly. Multi-column documents, pages with sidebars, floating text boxes, and wrapped figures create reading order ambiguity that OCR resolves unreliably. The output may interleave text from different columns or read a sidebar before the main content it sits beside.

4. OCR cannot extract semantic meaning

OCR tells you a document contains the text "$1,247.50." It cannot tell you that this number is the invoice total rather than a line-item price, a tax amount, or a shipping charge. Extracting semantic meaning from recognized text requires named entity recognition and field classification, which use both the text content and its spatial position to determine meaning.

5. OCR ignores non-text elements

Figures, charts, logos, signatures, stamps, barcodes, and diagrams are invisible to standard OCR because they do not contain recognizable characters. On documents where these visual elements carry important information (a signature confirming approval, a chart showing trends, a barcode containing a tracking number), OCR provides an incomplete picture of the document's content.

Open-Source OCR Engines in 2026

Three open-source OCR engines dominate the landscape. Each has distinct strengths.

Tesseract (version 5)

The most widely used open-source OCR engine. Maintained by Google until 2024, now community-maintained. Tesseract 5 uses LSTM-based recognition and supports over 100 languages. It produces multiple output formats (plain text, hOCR, TSV, ALTO XML, PDF with embedded text layer).

Strengths: Broad language support, extensive documentation, large community, multiple output formats, runs entirely on CPU.

Limitations: Accuracy drops on degraded scans and complex layouts. Preprocessing quality significantly affects results. No built-in table structure recognition.

PaddleOCR

Developed by Baidu. Supports 80+ languages with particularly strong accuracy on Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Arabic scripts. Includes a layout analysis module (PP-Structure) that goes beyond basic text recognition to detect tables, figures, and document regions.

Strengths: Best multilingual accuracy (especially non-Latin scripts), built-in layout analysis, table structure recognition, active development with frequent updates.

Limitations: Requires the PaddlePaddle framework (less mainstream than PyTorch or TensorFlow), documentation is primarily in Chinese with English translations.

EasyOCR

A Python library supporting 80+ languages with a simple API. Uses a CRNN (Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network) architecture for text recognition.

Strengths: Easiest API to use (two lines of Python to run OCR on an image), good accuracy on scene text (text in photographs, signage, product labels), supports GPU acceleration.

Limitations: Slower than Tesseract on large batches, smaller community, fewer output format options.

OCR vs Document AI: Where the Line Falls

The distinction between OCR and document AI is the distinction between reading characters and understanding documents.

OCR answers: what text is on this page?

Document AI answers: what text is on this page, what type of element is each piece of text, where exactly is each element, and what role does each element play in the document's structure?

In modern document processing pipelines, OCR is not an alternative to document AI. It is a layer within it. OCR provides the text recognition layer. Document AI provides the structural understanding layer that transforms raw text into structured, actionable data.

For intelligent document processing systems that extract specific fields from documents (invoice totals, contract dates, patient names), OCR is the necessary first step that makes the text available, and document AI is the necessary second step that makes the text meaningful.

How OCR Output Feeds Into ML Training

OCR output plays a specific role in training modern document AI models, particularly vision-language models that process both visual and textual information.

LayoutLMv3, one of the most widely used document AI models, takes three inputs simultaneously: text tokens, 2D position coordinates, and a page image. The text tokens and position coordinates come directly from OCR output. Each word recognized by OCR becomes a token, and its bounding box coordinates become the positional embedding that tells the model where that word appears on the page.

The accuracy of the OCR output directly affects the accuracy of the trained model. OCR errors in the training data become learned errors in the model. A word consistently misrecognized by OCR during training data preparation will be consistently mishandled by the model in production.

This is why OCR accuracy matters beyond the immediate text extraction use case. For teams building document AI models, OCR quality is a training data quality issue. And as research consistently shows, training data quality determines model performance more reliably than model architecture.

OCR-free models like Donut bypass this dependency by reading text directly from the page image without a separate OCR step. But OCR-dependent models like LayoutLMv3 remain widely used because they leverage the mature, high-accuracy text recognition that decades of OCR development have produced.

How to Choose the Right OCR Approach

The right OCR approach depends on your documents, your accuracy requirements, and what you plan to do with the extracted text.

If you need text extraction only (search indexing, archival, basic text mining), a standalone OCR engine (Tesseract or PaddleOCR) is sufficient. Run it on your documents, validate the accuracy on a sample, and integrate the text output into your application.

If you need structured field extraction (pulling specific values like invoice totals, contract dates, or patient names), OCR alone is not enough. You need OCR for the text layer plus document AI for the structural layer. The complete pipeline is described in our guide to document AI pipelines.

If your documents are multilingual (particularly non-Latin scripts), start with PaddleOCR. Its accuracy on Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Arabic significantly exceeds Tesseract.

If you are building training data for a document AI model, your OCR output quality matters as much as your label quality. Evaluate OCR accuracy on your specific documents before committing to a recognition engine. A 2% accuracy difference in OCR output compounds across thousands of training pages.

If your documents contain tables, OCR alone will not preserve the row-column structure. You need dedicated table extraction methods that understand grid layouts.

The Future of OCR

OCR is converging with document AI. The line between "reading characters" and "understanding documents" is blurring as new model architectures combine both capabilities.

Vision-language models like Donut and Pix2Struct perform OCR and document understanding in a single model, eliminating the separate OCR step entirely. These models read text directly from the page image and produce structured output without needing a separate OCR engine.

But traditional OCR is not going away. It remains faster, cheaper, and more accurate on clean printed text than any vision-language model. For high-volume text extraction where structural understanding is not needed, standalone OCR remains the right tool. For applications that need both text extraction and document understanding, the pipeline approach (OCR for text, document AI for structure) continues to produce the most reliable results.

The teams that build the strongest document processing systems in 2026 understand both technologies, know where each one excels, and combine them at the right points in the pipeline.
