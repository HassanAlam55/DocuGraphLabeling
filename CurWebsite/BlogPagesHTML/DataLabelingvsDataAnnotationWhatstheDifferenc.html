Data Labeling vs Data Annotation: What's the Difference?

If you have spent any time researching how to prepare training data for machine learning, you have probably noticed that "data labeling" and "data annotation" get used as if they mean the same thing. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they don't. And the confusion is not just semantic. Choosing the wrong approach for your project, or misunderstanding what a tool or service actually delivers, can waste weeks of work and produce training data that your model cannot use effectively.

This guide breaks down what each term actually means in practice, where they overlap, where they diverge, and how to decide which approach fits your project. We will pay particular attention to how these concepts apply to document AI and PDF processing, since that is the domain where the distinction has the most practical consequences.

The Short Answer

Data labeling is the process of assigning a category, tag, or class to a data point. It answers the question: what is this? A labeled image might be tagged as "invoice." A labeled text passage might be tagged as "positive sentiment." A labeled document region might be tagged as "table" or "header."

Data annotation is the process of adding any kind of structured metadata to raw data, including labels, but also spatial information, relationships between elements, contextual notes, and rich descriptive details. It answers a broader set of questions: what is this, where is it, what are its boundaries, and how does it relate to other elements?

The simplest way to think about it: all labeling is annotation, but not all annotation is labeling. Labeling is a specific type of annotation focused on classification. Annotation is the broader category that includes labeling plus spatial markup, segmentation, entity tagging, relationship mapping, and other forms of structured metadata.

Why the Confusion Exists

The terms get used interchangeably for a good reason: in many real-world workflows, you are doing both at the same time.

When you draw a bounding box around a table in a PDF and tag it as "table," you are performing annotation (drawing the box) and labeling (assigning the class) in a single action. When you highlight a paragraph in a document and classify it as "section_header," the highlighting is annotation and the classification is labeling. The two processes are so tightly coupled in practice that separating them feels artificial.

This is why most data labeling tools perform annotation as well. And why most data annotation platforms include labeling as a core feature. The tools do not enforce a distinction because the workflows do not require one in most cases.

The distinction matters when you need to be precise about what kind of training data your model requires. And that precision becomes critical as your project moves from prototyping to production.

Data Labeling in Practice

Data labeling focuses on classification. You take a data point and assign it to one or more predefined categories. The output is typically a simple mapping: this input belongs to this class.

What labeling looks like across data types

For images: The entire image gets a class label. "This image contains a dog." "This image is a chest X-ray." "This image is an invoice." No spatial information, no bounding boxes, no pixel-level detail. Just a classification applied to the whole input.

For text: A sentence, paragraph, or document gets a tag. "This review is positive." "This email is spam." "This support ticket is about billing." The label applies to the entire text unit without specifying which words or phrases drove the classification.

For documents: A full page or document gets a category. "This PDF is a contract." "This page is a financial statement." "This form is a W-2." Document-level classification is a labeling task because you are assigning one tag to the entire input. If you need to understand what is inside the document, such as where the tables are, where the headers start, or which regions contain signatures, you have moved beyond labeling into annotation territory.

For audio: A clip gets a classification. "This recording contains speech." "This audio sample is music." "This call is a complaint." The label describes the whole clip, not specific moments within it.

When labeling is enough

Labeling is the right choice when your model needs to sort inputs into categories without understanding their internal structure. Common use cases include:

Sentiment analysis, where the model classifies text as positive, negative, or neutral. Content moderation, where images or text are classified as safe or unsafe. Document routing, where incoming PDFs are sorted into categories like "invoice," "contract," or "report" before any extraction happens. Email classification, where messages are tagged by department, priority, or topic.

These tasks share a common trait: the model only needs to answer "what category does this belong to?" It does not need to locate specific elements within the input, draw boundaries, or understand spatial relationships.

What labeled output looks like

A labeled dataset for document classification might look like this in its simplest form:

{"file": "doc_001.pdf", "label": "invoice"}

{"file": "doc_002.pdf", "label": "contract"}

{"file": "doc_003.pdf", "label": "tax_form"}

Clean, simple, and sufficient for training a classifier. No coordinates, no spatial metadata, no element-level detail.

Data Annotation in Practice

Data annotation adds structured metadata to raw data. This includes classification labels, but it also includes spatial information (where things are), boundary information (the exact edges of each element), relationships between elements, and contextual attributes that go beyond simple categorization.

What annotation looks like across data types

For images: Instead of just labeling an image as "contains a car," annotation marks where the car is (bounding box), traces its exact outline (polygon or segmentation mask), identifies its parts (wheels, windows, license plate), and may even note its relationship to other objects ("car is behind pedestrian"). The output is far richer than a single class label.

For text: Instead of labeling a review as "positive," annotation marks specific entities ("iPhone 15" is a product, "Apple" is an organization), identifies relationships ("Apple" manufactures "iPhone 15"), and tags sentiment at the phrase level rather than the document level. This level of detail is what powers named entity recognition and relation extraction models.

For documents: This is where annotation becomes especially important and where the difference from labeling is most consequential. Document annotation involves identifying every structural element on a page, drawing bounding boxes around each one, and classifying each region by its role in the document. A single annotated PDF page might contain labels for headers, paragraphs, tables, figures, footers, page numbers, signatures, and captions, each with precise coordinates that tell the model exactly where that element starts and ends.

If you are building a model that needs to extract tables from PDFs or identify specific fields in a form, you need annotated data, not just labeled data. The model must learn both what each element is and where it is on the page.

For audio: Annotation marks specific time segments, speaker identity, emotional tone changes within the clip, and background noise classifications. Each timestamp gets its own metadata rather than the entire clip receiving a single label.

What annotated output looks like

The Annotation Types That Go Beyond Labeling

One of the biggest gaps in most comparisons of labeling versus annotation is that they treat annotation as a single thing. In reality, annotation encompasses several distinct techniques, each producing different kinds of training data for different kinds of models.

Bounding box annotation

Rectangular boxes drawn around elements of interest. This is the most common annotation type for document AI, object detection, and layout analysis. Each box captures the approximate location and size of an element but does not trace its exact outline.

Used for: document layout detection, object detection in images, UI element identification. Tools that auto-label PDF documents typically produce bounding box annotations as their primary output.

Polygon annotation

Irregular shapes traced around the exact boundaries of elements. More precise than bounding boxes but more time-consuming to produce. Polygons follow the actual contour of an object rather than enclosing it in a rectangle.

Used for: irregularly shaped objects, satellite imagery analysis, medical image segmentation, and documents with non-rectangular layouts.

Semantic segmentation

Every pixel in an image is classified into a category. Instead of drawing boxes or shapes around elements, the entire image is divided into regions where each pixel belongs to a specific class. No distinction is made between separate instances of the same class.

Used for: autonomous driving (road vs. sidewalk vs. building), medical imaging (tissue type classification), and document processing where precise region boundaries matter.

Instance segmentation

Similar to semantic segmentation, but each individual instance of an object gets its own identity. If there are three tables on a page, semantic segmentation labels all table pixels the same way. Instance segmentation identifies Table 1, Table 2, and Table 3 as separate objects.

Used for: counting objects, tracking individual elements across pages, and any task where knowing how many of something exists matters as much as knowing what it is.

Named entity recognition (NER)

Specific spans of text are tagged with entity types: person names, organizations, dates, monetary amounts, locations. NER is an annotation technique, not a labeling technique, because it marks specific positions within the text rather than classifying the text as a whole.

Used for: information extraction, contract analysis, resume parsing, and any text processing task that needs to pull specific data points from unstructured content.

Relationship annotation

Connections between annotated elements are explicitly marked. "This signature belongs to this signatory block." "This amount references this line item." "This annotation refers to this paragraph." Relationship annotation adds a layer of meaning that individual element labels cannot capture on their own.

Used for: knowledge graph construction, document understanding systems, form field mapping, and any task where understanding how elements connect matters.

Understanding which types of document annotation your project requires is one of the first decisions that shapes your entire ML pipeline.

How the Distinction Affects Your ML Pipeline

The choice between labeling and annotation does not just affect your training data. It cascades into every downstream decision in your ML pipeline.

Model architecture

Classification models (trained on labeled data) are architecturally different from detection and segmentation models (trained on annotated data). A document classifier that sorts PDFs into categories uses a different model architecture than a document layout analyzer that identifies and locates every element on a page. Choosing labeling when you need annotation means building a model that cannot do what your application requires.

Training data format

Labeled datasets are simple key-value pairs. Annotated datasets require structured formats with coordinate systems, element hierarchies, and metadata schemas. The JSON export format matters because your ML framework (PyTorch, TensorFlow, HuggingFace) expects data in specific structures. Labeled data and annotated data use different schemas, different data loaders, and different preprocessing pipelines.

Evaluation metrics

Classification models are evaluated with accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Detection and segmentation models are evaluated with mean Average Precision (mAP), Intersection over Union (IoU), and pixel accuracy. If you collect labeled data but your application requires detection-level evaluation, you will not be able to measure whether your model is actually working.

Annotation cost and timeline

Labeling is faster and cheaper. A human labeler can classify hundreds of documents per hour because each decision is a single categorical choice. Annotation is slower and more expensive because each document requires multiple spatial decisions, boundary judgments, and element classifications. This cost difference influences whether you can afford to annotate your entire dataset or need to use a combination of manual and automated approaches.

Data Labeling vs Data Annotation for Document AI

This is where the distinction matters most in 2026, and where most existing comparisons fall short. Document AI sits at the intersection of computer vision and natural language processing, and it requires both labeling and annotation at different stages of the pipeline.

Stage 1: Document classification (labeling)

Before you can extract information from a document, you often need to classify it. Is this PDF an invoice, a contract, a medical record, or a tax form? Document classification is a labeling task. The model receives a full document as input and outputs a single category.

If your pipeline starts with document sorting, you need labeled data for this stage. Each document gets one class label, no spatial annotation required.

Stage 2: Layout analysis (annotation)

Once you know what type of document you are working with, the next step is understanding its structure. Where are the headers? Where do the tables start and end? Which regions contain body text versus figures versus footers? This is a layout analysis task, and it requires annotated data with bounding boxes and element classifications.

This is the stage where choosing the right labeling taxonomy has the biggest impact. The categories you define for document elements, such as header, paragraph, table, list_item, figure, and footer, determine what your model can learn to detect.

Stage 3: Information extraction (annotation + NER)

After layout analysis identifies document regions, information extraction pulls specific values from those regions. The invoice total, the contract effective date, the patient name, the policy number. This stage combines spatial annotation (knowing where the field is) with entity recognition (knowing what kind of data it contains).

How auto-labeling tools handle both

Modern platforms blur the line between labeling and annotation by performing both simultaneously. When you upload a PDF to an auto-labeling tool, the system does not just classify the document. It segments the page into regions, draws bounding boxes around each element, assigns a structural label to each region, and exports the complete annotated dataset, all in one pass.

This is the practical reason why the labeling versus annotation distinction has become less important for many teams. The tools handle both, and the output includes everything downstream models need. The benefits of automated data labeling extend to both classification and spatial annotation, compressing what used to be separate manual workflows into a single automated step.

When the Distinction Does Not Matter

Here is something most articles on this topic will not tell you: for a significant number of real-world projects, the distinction between labeling and annotation is purely academic.

If you are using a tool that automatically segments and classifies document elements, you are getting both labeled and annotated data in one output. You do not need to decide between the two. You do not need to hire different teams for each. And you do not need to build separate pipelines.

The distinction matters when:

You are scoping a manual annotation project and need to estimate cost and timeline. Labeling is cheaper and faster; full spatial annotation costs more and takes longer. Planning around the right level of detail prevents budget overruns.

You are evaluating vendor services and need to understand what "labeled data" actually includes. Some vendors use "labeled" to mean fully annotated with bounding boxes. Others use it to mean classification tags only. Asking for a sample output before committing prevents expensive misunderstandings.

You are choosing between model architectures and need to know what kind of training data each requires. A classifier needs labels. A detector needs annotations. Building the wrong dataset for your chosen architecture wastes the entire data preparation effort.

You are designing a data annotation workflow from scratch and need to define the annotation schema, hire the right annotators, and select the right tools. Knowing whether you need classification labels, spatial annotations, or both determines every downstream decision.

The distinction does not matter when:

Your tool produces comprehensive output that includes both labels and spatial annotations. This is increasingly common with auto-labeling platforms that handle segmentation and classification together.

You are working with a small, well-defined dataset where the complexity of the annotation is obvious. If you are annotating 200 invoices for table extraction, you already know you need bounding boxes, and calling it "labeling" or "annotation" changes nothing about the work.

Common Misconceptions

"Data annotation is always more complex than data labeling." Not necessarily. Annotating images with simple bounding boxes around large, obvious objects can be faster and simpler than labeling ambiguous text for sentiment. Complexity depends on the task, not the technique.

"You have to choose one or the other." In practice, most document AI projects need both. Classification labels for document sorting, spatial annotations for layout analysis, and entity tags for information extraction. These are stages in a pipeline, not competing approaches.

"Automated labeling replaces annotation." Automated tools perform annotation, not just labeling. When an auto-labeling platform segments a PDF into regions with bounding boxes and classifications, it is producing annotated data. The automation replaces manual effort, not the annotation itself.

"More annotation detail always produces better models." Not always. Over-annotation can introduce noise, increase training time, and create label schemas that are too granular for the model to learn effectively. The right level of annotation detail matches the specificity your application actually needs.

Choosing the Right Approach for Your Project

Rather than defaulting to one approach, work backward from what your model needs to do.

If your model classifies entire documents into categories, you need labeled data. Document routing, spam detection, content moderation, and triage systems all fall into this category. Labels are faster to produce and cheaper to collect at scale.

If your model locates and identifies elements within documents, you need annotated data with bounding boxes and element classifications. Layout analysis, table extraction, form field detection, and document understanding all require spatial annotations that labeling alone cannot provide.

If your model extracts specific values from documents, you need annotated data that combines spatial markup with entity-level tagging. Information extraction, key-value pair extraction, and structured data output all require the richest form of annotation.

If you are not sure, start with annotation. A fully annotated dataset can always be reduced to labels by dropping the spatial information. A labeled dataset cannot be upgraded to annotations without going back to the source documents and adding bounding boxes from scratch. Annotating upfront preserves your options for every downstream use case.

Summary

Data labeling and data annotation are related but distinct processes. Labeling assigns categorical tags to entire data points. Annotation adds structured metadata including labels, spatial coordinates, boundaries, and relationships. In document AI, you typically need both: labels for document classification and annotations for layout analysis and information extraction.

The practical distinction matters most when scoping projects, evaluating tools, choosing model architectures, and estimating costs. For teams using modern auto-labeling platforms that produce comprehensive annotated output in a single pass, the terminology debate becomes less important than the quality and completeness of the training data itself.

The right question is not "do I need labeling or annotation?" The right question is "what level of detail does my model need to learn from, and what is the fastest way to produce that training data at the quality my application requires?"
