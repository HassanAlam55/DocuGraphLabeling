How to Build a RAG Pipeline from Unstructured PDFs

Most Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipelines built on PDFs do not fail at retrieval. They do not fail at the LLM either. They fail at ingestion, the exact moment a messy PDF gets dumped into a text splitter and comes out the other end as noise. Tables split mid-row. Section headers disappear. Answers surface with the right words but the wrong numbers. And the fix is almost never a better chunking algorithm.

The fix is structure. Before anything gets chunked, embedded, or retrieved, the PDF needs to be parsed with its layout intact. This guide walks through the full RAG pipeline for PDF-based knowledge bases the way production ML teams build it in 2026, from raw document intake through evaluation. Every stage that beginner guides skip gets its own section here: layout-aware ingestion, structure-first chunking, metadata enrichment, hybrid retrieval, reranking, table handling, and the evaluation metrics that tell you whether any of it is actually working.

What Is a RAG Pipeline?

Retrieval-Augmented Generation combines two components: a retrieval system that finds relevant information from an external knowledge base, and a generation system (a large language model) that uses the retrieved information to produce grounded answers.

The value proposition is simple. Base LLMs know a lot about the world at large but nothing about your specific documents, your customers, your contracts, or your internal knowledge. Fine-tuning is one way to inject that knowledge. RAG is the faster, cheaper, more auditable alternative. Instead of training the model on your data, you retrieve the right passages at query time and feed them to the model as context.

When done well, a RAG system gives you three things a base LLM cannot: answers grounded in your actual documents, citations back to the source, and the ability to update the knowledge base without retraining anything.

The Anatomy of a PDF RAG Pipeline

A production-grade RAG pipeline for PDF-based knowledge bases has seven distinct stages. Skipping any of them is where quality problems come from.

●        Ingestion. Extract text, tables, figures, and structure from source PDFs while preserving layout information.

●        Chunking. Split the extracted content into retrievable units that respect document structure.

●        Metadata enrichment. Attach page numbers, section paths, document IDs, confidence scores, and access-control tags to every chunk.

●        Embedding. Convert each chunk into a dense vector representation using an embedding model.

●        Indexing and storage. Store vectors and metadata in a vector database designed for approximate nearest neighbor search.

●        Retrieval. At query time, find the chunks most relevant to the user's question, optionally combining dense search, sparse search, and reranking.

●        Generation. Assemble the retrieved chunks into a prompt and generate the final answer, ideally with citations back to source.

The order matters. Every downstream stage inherits the quality of the ones before it, and the compounding effect of a weak ingestion stage is what kills most PDF RAG systems.

Why PDFs Break Standard RAG Pipelines

The default RAG tutorial pipeline works well on plain text. Feed it a Wikipedia dump and it will happily embed, index, and retrieve. Feed it a real enterprise PDF corpus and it will underperform in ways that are hard to diagnose.

Three problems are responsible for most of the pain:

Layout carries meaning that raw text loses. A financial statement is not a linear sequence of words. Column position, table structure, and header hierarchy encode information that disappears the moment you extract plain text with a naive PDF library. Once the structure is gone, no downstream chunker or embedder can put it back.

Tables get destroyed by generic extraction. Standard PDF extractors turn a clean two-column table with merged headers into a garbled string of numbers and labels. Any question that requires reading that table correctly will return a wrong answer, no matter how good your retrieval or LLM is.

Chunk boundaries fall in the wrong places. Fixed-length chunking cuts through sentences, paragraphs, sections, and even individual table rows. A single answer often ends up split across two chunks, neither of which contains the full context needed to answer the question.

Stage 1: Layout-Aware PDF Ingestion

This is the stage that decides whether the rest of your pipeline stands a chance. The goal is to convert each PDF into a structured representation that preserves headings, paragraphs, tables, figures, list items, and reading order, with bounding box coordinates and page numbers attached to every element.

A layout-aware extractor produces structured output that typically includes:

●        A hierarchical document structure (document → sections → subsections → paragraphs)

●        Element type labels using a standard taxonomy such as PubLayNet or DocBank (page_header, page_footer, section_header, text, table, list_item, figure)

●        Bounding box coordinates in [x1, y1, x2, y2] format for every element

●        Page numbers and reading order

●        Table content in a structured format (rows and cells preserved, not flattened)

●        Confidence scores for uncertain regions

Stage 2: Structure-First Chunking

Once you have structured extraction, chunking becomes a very different problem. Instead of counting characters and hoping for the best, you split on document structure the extractor already identified.

The Chunking Strategies That Actually Work

Structural (heading-aware) chunking. Split at every section header, then further at subheaders if the resulting chunks are too long. This is the strongest general-purpose default when your extractor exposes headings. Chunks correspond to logical document units, which is what both retrieval and human intuition expect.

Semantic chunking. Use embeddings to detect semantic shifts within a document and place chunk boundaries where topic changes occur. More expensive to compute but useful when your documents lack strong heading structure. A NAACL 2025 Findings paper reported that fixed 200-word chunks matched or beat semantic chunking on many retrieval tasks, so measure before you invest here.

Hierarchical (parent-child) chunking. Store multiple chunk levels for the same document: fine-grained chunks for precise retrieval, and coarser parent chunks for broader context. At retrieval time, match on the fine chunks but return the parent chunk (or both) to the LLM. This is one of the highest-impact upgrades you can make to a basic RAG system.

Page-level chunking. Treat each page as one chunk. Simple, and useful when your documents are visually organized page by page (financial reports, scientific papers with figures). Weak when the meaningful units cross page boundaries.

Fixed-size chunking with overlap. The default baseline. Split into 500-1000 token chunks with 10-20 percent overlap between consecutive chunks to preserve context at boundaries. Beat this before you consider it good enough.

Chunk Size Guidance

There is no universal correct chunk size. Practical starting points:

●        General text: 300-800 tokens per chunk with 50-100 token overlap

●        Technical documentation: 500-1500 tokens per chunk with heading-aware boundaries

●        Legal and contract text: full clause or full section per chunk, no arbitrary length limit

●        Financial statements: full table per chunk, plus a summary chunk for context

Measure retrieval quality across two or three configurations on your own data. The differences are real and worth the experiment time.

Stage 3: Metadata Enrichment

Every chunk should carry rich metadata alongside its text. Metadata drives filtered retrieval, access control, citation quality, and evaluation. The chunk itself is what the LLM sees; the metadata is what makes the retrieval useful.

Standard metadata fields for a chunk in a document RAG system:

●        document_id (unique identifier for the source PDF)

●        document_title (human-readable name)

●        page_number (or page range if the chunk spans pages)

●        section_path (breadcrumb of headings: 'Contract > Section 4 > Termination')

●        element_type (paragraph, table, list, figure caption)

●        bbox (bounding box coordinates for visual grounding and citation)

●        document_type (contract, financial statement, research paper, insurance claim)

●        confidence (from the extraction stage, useful for filtering and reranking)

●        effective_date or version (for time-sensitive documents like contracts and policies)

●        access_tags (used to enforce access control at retrieval time)

The choice of taxonomy for element_type and document_type is not cosmetic. It shapes what queries can be filtered on and what evaluation slices are possible. Stage 4: Embedding and Vector Storage

Embedding converts each chunk into a dense numerical vector that captures its semantic meaning. Two chunks about the same topic will have similar vectors, even if they use different words.

Choosing an Embedding Model

The choice of embedding model has as much impact on retrieval quality as your chunking strategy. Consider four factors:

●        Dimension. Common sizes are 384, 768, 1024, and 3072. Higher-dimensional vectors capture more nuance but cost more to store and search.

●        Context window. Some embedding models truncate inputs at 512 tokens; others handle 8K or more. Match this to your chunk size, or your chunks get silently cropped.

●        Domain. General-purpose embedding models perform well on general text but often lose accuracy on specialized domains like law, medicine, or finance. Domain-tuned models exist and are worth evaluating.

●        Cost and hosting. Hosted API embeddings are simple but add latency and per-token cost. Self-hosted sentence-transformer models are free after the compute investment and give you full control over versioning.

Vector Store Selection

A vector store handles approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search across millions or billions of vectors with sub-second latency. Modern options include Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Milvus, Chroma, and FAISS. The main axes of choice:

●        Managed service vs self-hosted

●        Metadata filtering support (essential for enterprise RAG)

●        Hybrid dense + sparse search support (important for numeric and named-entity queries)

●        Scale (millions vs billions of vectors)

●        Update semantics (streaming inserts vs batch reindexing)

For most production RAG systems on PDFs, the vector store choice matters less than the ingestion and chunking choices upstream of it. Get the pipeline working with a simple option first, then swap in a more sophisticated store when scale demands it.

Stage 5: Retrieval Strategies That Beat the Default

The default retrieval strategy in most tutorials is single-shot dense retrieval: embed the query, do a nearest neighbor search, return the top K chunks. This works, but the upgrades that follow are where production RAG systems earn their reputation.

Hybrid Search (Dense + Sparse)

Dense embeddings are excellent at semantic similarity but weak at exact-match queries. Ask a dense retriever for 'invoice number INV-2024-8871' and it will happily return semantically similar chunks that do not contain that string. Sparse retrieval (BM25) is the opposite: strong on exact matches, weak on paraphrase.

Combining both, then fusing the results with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) or a learned weighting, gives you the strengths of each and covers the failure mode of both. Hybrid search is now the default in serious production RAG systems.

Reranking with a Cross-Encoder

First-stage retrieval optimizes for recall: get the right chunks into a shortlist of 50-100. A cross-encoder then reranks that shortlist for precision, scoring each candidate chunk against the query using a model that sees both together. The reranked top 5-10 chunks are what actually go to the LLM.

Cross-encoder reranking adds latency (each candidate is a separate model call) but the accuracy gain is consistently large enough to justify the cost in production systems.

Query Transformation

User queries are frequently underspecified, ambiguous, or badly worded for retrieval. Query transformation strategies rewrite or expand the query before retrieval:

●        Query decomposition. Break a complex multi-part query into sub-queries, retrieve for each, and merge results.

●        HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings). Have the LLM generate a hypothetical answer to the query, then embed and retrieve using that answer instead of the query. Often improves recall on hard queries.

●        Multi-query retrieval. Generate several rewrites of the query, retrieve for each, and union the results.

●        Contextual query rewriting. Rewrite follow-up questions in a conversation to be self-contained ('What about the second one?' → 'What is the effective date of the second contract?').

Metadata Filtering

For any real enterprise use case, retrieval should filter by metadata before or during the vector search. Restrict to a specific document type, a date range, a business unit, or an access-controlled subset. Without metadata filtering, a 10-million-chunk index will drown any query in irrelevant matches from documents the user should not even be looking at.

Stage 6: Prompt Construction and Generation

The retrieved chunks now need to be assembled into a prompt the LLM can act on. The details of this stage are the difference between an answer that reads well and an answer that is actually correct.

Prompt Structure

A well-structured RAG prompt has four sections:

●        System instructions. Define the model's role, the answer format, and the rules for handling missing information ('If the context does not contain the answer, say so').

●        Retrieved context. The top chunks, each preceded by a citation marker (chunk ID, document, page, section).

●        User query. The original question, verbatim.

●        Output format guidance. Instructions for how to cite sources, format tables, or structure the response.

Citation and Grounding

Every answer produced by a production RAG system should cite its sources. Cite at the chunk level using the metadata attached in Stage 3: document title, page number, section path. If your extractor preserved bounding boxes, you can even highlight the exact region on the source page that produced the answer, which is the gold standard for auditable enterprise AI.

Context Window Management

Modern LLMs have context windows of 100K tokens or more, but that does not mean you should fill them. Longer context increases latency, cost, and the well-documented 'lost in the middle' effect where models pay less attention to information in the middle of a long context. The typical sweet spot is 5-10 well-chosen chunks totaling 3,000-8,000 tokens.

Stage 7: Evaluation That Tells You What Is Actually Broken

A RAG system that is not evaluated is a system that will fail silently in production. Build the evaluation harness alongside the pipeline, not after.

Component-Level Metrics

Retrieval metrics. Recall@K, precision@K, mean reciprocal rank (MRR), and normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG) on a labeled test set of query-to-relevant-chunk pairs. Build this test set with a mix of manually curated examples and LLM-generated synthetic queries validated by humans.

Generation metrics. Faithfulness (does the answer contain information not in the retrieved context?), answer relevance (does the answer address the query?), and context precision (were the retrieved chunks actually used?).

End-to-end metrics. Answer accuracy on a labeled QA dataset, human preference ratings, and task-specific business metrics (customer satisfaction, ticket deflection rate, time to answer).

Evaluation Frameworks

The RAGAS framework has become the de facto standard for automated RAG evaluation. It computes faithfulness, context precision, context recall, and answer relevance using LLM-as-judge. Combine it with a manually labeled evaluation set for the queries that matter most to your business.

Handling Tables and Figures in RAG

Tables are where most PDF RAG systems fail hardest. They contain the numbers, the pricing, the specifications, the terms of the deal, the exact data users are trying to retrieve. And a generic PDF extractor turns them into unreadable text.

The right approach is to treat tables as first-class citizens throughout the pipeline. Extract them with structure intact using a layout-aware system (rows and cells preserved), store them separately from paragraph text, and index them with rich metadata that describes what the table contains. Our deep-dive on extracting tables from PDFs with machine learning covers the technical options in detail. Once tables are extracted correctly, three strategies work well for retrieval:

●        Table as chunk. Store each table as its own chunk in Markdown or CSV format. Simple and often sufficient for small tables.

●        Table summary + table. Generate an LLM summary of each table describing what it contains, embed the summary for retrieval, and pass the full table to the generator when the summary matches. This is more expensive but dramatically better on large tables.

●        Structured table storage. For heavily tabular corpora (financial filings, regulatory data), store tables in a relational database or DataFrame alongside the vector store. Route table-shaped queries to SQL and prose queries to vector retrieval.

Figures and diagrams are the frontier. Multi-modal RAG systems that can embed figure images alongside text chunks are now viable but still complex to build. For most document RAG systems in 2026, extracting the figure caption and treating it as a text chunk (with a link to the source image) captures most of the value at a fraction of the complexity.

Domain-Specific Considerations

Different document domains have different failure modes and different chunk-and-retrieve requirements.

Legal. Contracts are highly structured, with numbered sections, defined terms, and internal cross-references. Chunk at the clause level, preserve section paths in metadata, and consider a definitions index that expands defined terms at retrieval time.

Financial. Statements and filings are table-dominated. Prioritize table extraction quality over paragraph chunking. Version and effective-date metadata are critical because a 2024 balance sheet is not a 2026 balance sheet, and retrieving the wrong period is worse than retrieving nothing.

Medical. HIPAA compliance requires PII redaction before ingestion, access-controlled chunk retrieval, and auditable citation back to the source record. Never let a RAG system return raw patient data across access boundaries.

Insurance and claims. Documents mix structured forms, free-text narratives, and scanned attachments. Use metadata to route each element type to the right retrieval strategy: structured fields to metadata filtering, narratives to dense retrieval, attachments to their own indexed sub-chunks.

Technical documentation. Preserve heading hierarchy in chunk metadata; users often search for a specific procedure by name. Hierarchical chunking with parent-child relationships pays off here more than almost anywhere else.

Production Considerations

Incremental Indexing

Real document collections change: new PDFs arrive, old ones get versioned, some get deleted. Build the pipeline to handle updates from day one. Every chunk should have a document_id and a version, and the vector store should support deletes and updates without a full reindex. Batch reindexing every night is not incremental indexing, and it will not scale.

Caching

Cache aggressively. Embedding calls, retrieval results, and even LLM completions can all be cached keyed on the query and a version tag for the underlying index. Well-designed caching cuts both latency and cost by an order of magnitude on repeated or similar queries.

Security and Access Control

Enterprise RAG systems must enforce document-level access control. Two patterns work in practice: filter chunks at retrieval time using access_tags in the metadata, or maintain per-user index shards. The filtering approach is simpler and scales further. Whichever you choose, test it explicitly: an unauthorized chunk in the LLM context is a data breach.

Observability

Log every stage of every query: the raw query, the transformed query, the retrieved chunks with their scores, the final prompt, the generated answer, and the user's implicit or explicit feedback. This log is what makes debugging possible when quality degrades in production. Without it, you are guessing.

Cost Management

The cost profile of a RAG system is heavily weighted toward two stages: embedding (one-time at ingestion, ongoing on new documents) and LLM generation (per query, forever). Optimize embedding cost with a smaller model where accuracy allows; optimize generation cost with smaller retrieval context, cheaper models for easy queries, and caching.

Common Pitfalls (And How to Avoid Them)

Skipping layout-aware ingestion. The single biggest quality win in PDF RAG is doing the ingestion stage properly. Automate it with a labeling engine that preserves structure, do not settle for raw text extraction.

Fixed chunking without measuring. Fixed-length chunks are a fine baseline but a bad final answer. Measure retrieval quality against at least one structural chunking alternative.

No metadata. A chunk without metadata is a chunk you cannot filter, cite, or version. Attach metadata at ingestion, not later.

Dense retrieval only. Add BM25 or another sparse retriever. It is almost always a win, especially for queries that mention specific names, numbers, or codes.

No reranking. Cross-encoder reranking on a shortlist of 50-100 candidates is one of the highest ROI upgrades available. Skipping it leaves accuracy on the table.

No evaluation harness. If you cannot measure retrieval and generation quality, you cannot improve them. Build the eval set before you scale the pipeline.

Ignoring tables. If your documents contain tables and you have not built a specific handling strategy for them, you have a large silent quality problem.

Manual labeling for scale. Any RAG pipeline with more than a few hundred source PDFs will underperform if labeling and annotation are done by hand. Our comparison of manual vs automated document labeling covers the accuracy, consistency, and cost trade-offs in detail.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between fine-tuning and RAG?

Fine-tuning updates the model's weights on your data. RAG retrieves your data at query time and passes it to the model as context. Fine-tuning is better for style, format, and deep behavioral changes. RAG is better for factual knowledge, recency, and citability. Most production systems combine both.

How large should chunks be in a PDF RAG system?

Start with 500-800 tokens per chunk and 50-100 token overlap. If your extractor exposes section headings, chunk on those instead. Measure retrieval quality on a labeled test set and adjust.

Do I need a vector database, or can I use a regular database?

For a few thousand chunks, a regular database with a similarity function works fine. Beyond roughly 100,000 chunks, use a purpose-built vector database. The performance gap grows steeply with scale.

Which embedding model should I start with?

For general text in English, current-generation OpenAI embeddings or an open-source sentence-transformer like BGE or E5 are reasonable starting points. For domain-specific corpora, evaluate a domain-tuned model before committing.

How do I handle scanned PDFs?

Run OCR first, then treat the OCR output as raw text input to the layout-aware extraction stage. Modern OCR from Tesseract or paid alternatives handles most scanned documents adequately. Watch for extraction errors on low-quality scans and consider human review of low-confidence regions before indexing.

How much does a production RAG system cost to run?

The dominant cost drivers are embedding (one-time per document, plus updates) and LLM generation (per query). At small scale (thousands of queries per month), expect costs in the low hundreds of dollars monthly with hosted APIs. At high scale (millions of queries), self-hosted embedding models and careful caching become important.

Can I use a RAG pipeline without a vector database at all?

Yes, for small corpora. Sparse retrieval (BM25) alone can be surprisingly competitive on small, well-structured document sets, and it requires no embeddings or vector store. Add dense retrieval when scale or query diversity demands it.

Putting It All Together

A production RAG pipeline for PDFs is not a single library call, and it is not a weekend project. What it is, once you accept that, is a structured problem with well-understood stages, each of which has known best practices and known failure modes.

The order is worth repeating: extract with layout intact, chunk on structure, enrich with metadata, embed with a model that fits your domain, index in a vector store that supports metadata filtering, retrieve with a hybrid strategy and rerank the shortlist, construct prompts that force citation and grounding, generate with a model appropriately sized for the query, and evaluate every stage with metrics that map to what your users actually need.
