What Is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)?

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, is a technique that makes large language models (LLMs) more accurate and useful by connecting them to external data sources. Instead of relying only on what the model learned during training, RAG retrieves relevant information from a knowledge base and feeds it into the model alongside the user's question. The model then generates a response grounded in that retrieved content.

The result is an AI system that can answer questions using your actual documents, databases, and knowledge repositories rather than depending on general training data that may be outdated, incomplete, or entirely wrong for your specific use case.

Patrick Lewis and his team at Meta AI (then Facebook AI Research) introduced the term in a 2020 research paper that demonstrated how combining retrieval with generation produced more factual, more specific, and more verifiable outputs than generation alone. Since then, RAG has become the dominant architecture for enterprise AI applications that need to work with proprietary data.

Why RAG Exists: The Core Problem It Solves

Large language models are trained on massive datasets, typically scraped from the public internet. That training gives them broad knowledge about the world, but it also gives them three fundamental limitations.

Their knowledge has a cutoff date. An LLM trained on data through 2024 knows nothing about events, products, regulations, or research published after that point. It cannot tell you about last quarter's earnings report or a policy change announced last month.

They cannot access your private data. Your company's contracts, internal documentation, customer records, product specifications, and operational procedures are not in the training data. The model has never seen them and cannot reason about them.

They hallucinate with confidence. When an LLM does not know the answer to a question, it often generates a plausible-sounding response anyway. These hallucinations are delivered with the same tone and structure as factual answers, making them difficult to detect without independent verification.

RAG addresses all three problems by giving the model access to current, relevant, and domain-specific information at the moment it generates a response. The model does not need to "know" the answer from training. It needs to find the answer in a knowledge base and use it correctly.

How RAG Works: The Three-Stage Pipeline

Every RAG system follows the same core architecture, regardless of the specific tools or models involved. The pipeline has three stages: indexing, retrieval, and generation.

Stage 1: Indexing (preparing the knowledge base)

Before a RAG system can retrieve anything, the source documents need to be prepared and stored in a searchable format. This stage handles the transformation from raw files into a structured knowledge base.

Document ingestion. Source materials are collected from wherever they live: file servers, cloud storage, content management systems, databases, or direct uploads. These can be PDFs, Word documents, HTML pages, Markdown files, spreadsheets, or any other text-bearing format.

Parsing and extraction. Raw documents are converted into clean text. For digital-native files, this is straightforward. For scanned documents, OCR (optical character recognition) converts images to text first. For complex PDFs with tables, headers, footers, and multi-column layouts, proper document parsing is critical because the structure of the original document affects the quality of everything downstream.

Chunking. The extracted text is split into smaller segments called chunks. Chunk size is one of the most important decisions in a RAG pipeline. Chunks that are too large become too general for specific queries. Chunks that are too small lose the context that makes them meaningful.

Common chunking strategies include fixed-size chunking (splitting every 500 tokens), sentence-based chunking, paragraph-based chunking, and semantic chunking (splitting at natural topic boundaries). The right strategy depends on the structure and purpose of your documents.

Embedding. Each chunk is converted into a numerical vector representation using an embedding model. These vectors capture the semantic meaning of each chunk in a format that allows mathematical comparison. Two chunks about similar topics will have vectors that are close together in the embedding space, even if they use completely different words.

Storage. The vectors are stored in a vector database (such as Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma, Qdrant, or pgvector) that is optimized for fast similarity search. The original text of each chunk is stored alongside its vector so it can be retrieved and passed to the model.

Stage 2: Retrieval (finding relevant information)

When a user submits a query, the retrieval stage finds the chunks most likely to contain the answer.

Query embedding. The user's question is converted into a vector using the same embedding model that encoded the chunks. This ensures that the query and the stored chunks exist in the same mathematical space.

Similarity search. The system searches the vector database for chunks whose vectors are closest to the query vector. "Closest" is typically measured using cosine similarity or dot product distance. The top-k most similar chunks are selected for the next stage.

Optional reranking. Some RAG systems add a reranking step where a cross-encoder model rescores the retrieved chunks based on more detailed relevance assessment. Reranking is slower than initial retrieval but produces more accurate results, especially for complex or ambiguous queries.

The retrieval stage is where most RAG failures originate. If the system retrieves irrelevant chunks, the model generates answers based on wrong information. If it retrieves too few chunks, the model lacks context. If it retrieves too many, important information gets buried in noise.

Stage 3: Generation (producing the response)

The retrieved chunks are combined with the user's original query into a prompt that is sent to the LLM. The prompt typically follows a pattern like:

"Using only the following context, answer the user's question. If the answer is not in the context, say you don't know."

[Retrieved chunks inserted here]

"User question: [original query]"

The LLM reads both the question and the retrieved context, then generates a response that draws on the provided information. Because the model is answering based on specific retrieved content rather than general training knowledge, the response is more likely to be factual, specific, and relevant to the user's actual question.

Many RAG systems also include citation or attribution, where the generated response indicates which source documents contributed to each claim. This makes it possible for users to verify the answer by checking the original sources.

What Makes or Breaks a RAG System: Document Quality

Here is the part that most RAG guides skip, and it is the part that determines whether your system actually works in production.

RAG quality is a function of retrieval quality. Retrieval quality is a function of chunk quality. And chunk quality is a function of how well your documents were parsed, structured, and segmented before they entered the pipeline.

A poorly parsed PDF produces garbled chunks. A table that was flattened into a single text string loses its row-column relationships. A header that was separated from its associated content creates a chunk that means nothing on its own. A footer repeated on every page pollutes the vector database with redundant, irrelevant content.

This is why document AI is not a separate discipline from RAG. It is the foundation that RAG quality depends on. Teams that treat document parsing as a solved problem and focus all their attention on retrieval algorithms and prompt engineering consistently underperform teams that invest in understanding their document structure first.

Tables are the hardest problem

Tables are where most RAG pipelines quietly fail. A financial statement, an invoice line-item breakdown, or a comparison chart contains structured information that loses its meaning entirely when converted to flat text.

Consider a simple two-column table:

| Product    | Price  |

|------------|--------|

| Widget A   | $14.99 |

| Widget B   | $22.50 |

If the parser flattens this into "Product Price Widget A $14.99 Widget B $22.50," a user asking "What does Widget B cost?" might get the right answer by luck. But a user asking "Which product is cheaper?" will get unreliable results because the flat text does not preserve the row-column relationships that make comparison possible.

Properly extracting tables from PDFs before they enter the RAG pipeline is not optional for any system that processes structured documents.

Headers and sections matter for chunking

When documents are chunked without understanding their structure, section headers often end up in separate chunks from the content they describe. This creates retrieval problems because the header chunk matches queries about the topic, but the actual information is in a different chunk entirely.

Structure-aware chunking, where the document's layout is analyzed before splitting, produces chunks that keep headers and their associated content together. This is one of the practical reasons why document labeling (identifying which regions are headers, which are body text, which are tables) improves RAG performance even though labeling is technically a separate task from retrieval.

RAG vs Fine-Tuning vs Prompt Engineering

RAG is not the only way to make an LLM work with specific data. Fine-tuning and prompt engineering are alternatives, and understanding the tradeoffs helps you choose the right approach.

Prompt engineering

The simplest approach. You paste relevant context directly into the prompt alongside your question. No infrastructure required, no vector database, no preprocessing pipeline.

Works when: Your context fits within the model's context window (typically 8K to 128K tokens depending on the model) and you know exactly which document contains the answer.

Breaks when: You have too much data to fit in a single prompt, you do not know which document is relevant, or you need the system to work without a human selecting the right context each time.

Fine-tuning

You retrain the model (or a layer of the model) on your specific data so it "learns" your domain knowledge. The information becomes part of the model's parameters.

Works when: You need the model to adopt a specific style, tone, or domain vocabulary. Also useful for teaching the model task-specific behavior like structured extraction or classification.

Breaks when: Your data changes frequently (you would need to retrain), you need the model to cite specific sources (fine-tuned knowledge is not attributable), or you have limited training data.

RAG

You keep the model unchanged and connect it to an external knowledge base that it searches at inference time.

Works when: Your data changes regularly, you need source attribution, you have large volumes of documents, or you need to control exactly what information the model can access.

Breaks when: Your documents are poorly structured, your queries require multi-step reasoning across many documents, or retrieval consistently fails to surface the right chunks.

The practical answer

Most production systems combine approaches. Fine-tuning teaches the model how to behave. RAG gives it access to current, specific information. Prompt engineering structures the interaction. They are not competing techniques. They are layers that serve different purposes.

Common RAG Failure Modes

Understanding where RAG fails is as important as understanding how it works. These are the patterns that cause production RAG systems to generate wrong, incomplete, or misleading answers.

Retrieval misses

The system retrieves chunks that are semantically similar to the query but do not actually contain the answer. This happens when the embedding model confuses topical similarity with informational relevance. A query about "quarterly revenue growth" might retrieve chunks that discuss revenue in general without containing the specific growth figures the user asked about.

Context window overflow

When the system retrieves too many chunks, the total context exceeds what the model can effectively process. Even models with large context windows (128K tokens or more) show degraded performance when the relevant information is buried in the middle of a long context. Research consistently shows that LLMs attend more strongly to information at the beginning and end of the prompt than to information in the middle.

Chunk boundary problems

Critical information split across two chunks may never be fully retrieved. If the answer to a question starts at the end of one chunk and continues at the beginning of the next, the system may retrieve only one of them, giving the model an incomplete picture. Overlapping chunks (where consecutive chunks share some content) partially address this but increase storage and retrieval costs.

Hallucination despite retrieval

RAG reduces hallucination but does not eliminate it. The model can still generate information that is not in the retrieved context, especially when the context is ambiguous, contradictory, or insufficient. Strong prompt design (explicitly instructing the model to only use provided context) helps, but no current approach fully prevents this.

Stale or contradictory sources

If the knowledge base contains multiple versions of the same document, or outdated information alongside current information, the retrieval system may surface conflicting content. The model then has to resolve the contradiction, which it often does poorly or arbitrarily.

Advanced RAG Patterns

The basic RAG pipeline described above is sometimes called "naive RAG." Production systems in 2026 typically incorporate one or more advanced patterns to improve performance.

Hybrid retrieval

Combines vector similarity search with traditional keyword search (BM25). Keyword search catches exact matches that embedding models sometimes miss, while vector search catches semantic matches that keyword search cannot find. Running both in parallel and merging the results produces more reliable retrieval than either approach alone.

Query transformation

Before searching the knowledge base, the system rewrites or expands the user's query to improve retrieval. Techniques include hypothetical document generation (HyDE), where the model generates a hypothetical answer and uses it as the search query, and query decomposition, where a complex question is broken into simpler sub-queries that are each searched independently.

GraphRAG

Instead of retrieving flat text chunks, GraphRAG retrieves subgraphs from a knowledge graph. Entities and their relationships are stored as nodes and edges, allowing the system to follow chains of connections that flat retrieval cannot represent. This is particularly powerful for multi-hop reasoning, where answering a question requires connecting information from multiple sources.

Agentic RAG

The retrieval system is wrapped in an agent framework that can make multiple retrieval calls, evaluate the quality of retrieved results, decide whether to search again with a modified query, and synthesize information from multiple retrieval rounds before generating a final answer. Agentic RAG handles complex questions better than single-pass retrieval but adds latency and cost.

Self-reflective RAG (Self-RAG and CRAG)

The model evaluates its own generated output for factual consistency with the retrieved context. If it detects that part of its response is not supported by the sources, it can trigger additional retrieval or modify its answer. This creates a feedback loop that improves answer quality at the cost of additional inference steps.

How Document Preprocessing Connects to RAG Quality

The connection between document structure and RAG performance is direct and measurable. Every improvement in how documents are parsed, segmented, and labeled translates into better chunks, better retrieval, and better generated answers.

This is where document AI tools play a role that is often overlooked in RAG architecture discussions. Before a document enters the RAG pipeline, it needs to be understood structurally: which regions are headers, which are body text, which are tables, which are figures, and which are metadata like page numbers and footers.

When this structural understanding is done well, the chunking stage can make intelligent decisions. Headers stay with their content. Tables are preserved as structured data rather than flattened text. Footers and repeated boilerplate are excluded from the knowledge base. The result is a cleaner, more relevant set of chunks that retrieval can actually work with.

It segments documents into labeled regions with bounding box coordinates, classifying each region by its role in the document. This output can feed directly into a RAG preprocessing pipeline, ensuring that the document taxonomy is correct before chunking begins.

When to Use RAG (and When Not To)

RAG is the right architecture when:

You need the AI to answer questions from a specific set of documents that were not in its training data. Internal knowledge bases, policy documents, product documentation, legal contracts, and financial reports all fit this pattern.

Your source data changes over time. RAG does not require retraining when documents are updated. You update the knowledge base, and the next retrieval automatically uses the new content.

You need source attribution. RAG can point to the specific document and passage that informed each part of its answer. This is essential in regulated industries where decisions must be traceable.

You have more data than fits in a single prompt. Any corpus larger than the model's context window needs retrieval to surface the right information for each query.

RAG is not the right architecture when:

The task is about style, format, or behavior rather than knowledge. Teaching a model to write in your brand voice or follow a specific output format is a fine-tuning problem, not a retrieval problem.

The answer requires real-time computation or external API calls. RAG retrieves static content from a knowledge base. It does not execute code, query live databases, or call external services (although agentic frameworks can add these capabilities alongside RAG).

The entire knowledge base is small enough to fit in context. If your corpus is a few thousand words, pasting it into the prompt is simpler, faster, and more reliable than building a full RAG pipeline.

The RAG Stack in 2026

A typical production RAG system in 2026 uses the following components:

Document parsing: Tools that convert raw PDFs, Word documents, and other files into structured, clean text while preserving document layout. This is the preprocessing layer that directly affects chunk quality.

Embedding models: Models that convert text into vector representations. Popular options include OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large, Cohere's embed-v4, and open-source models like BGE, GTE, and E5.

Vector databases: Purpose-built databases optimized for similarity search across millions of vectors. Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Chroma, Milvus, and pgvector are widely used.

Orchestration frameworks: Libraries that connect the components into a working pipeline. LangChain, LlamaIndex, and Haystack are the most established, though many teams build custom orchestration for production deployments.

LLMs for generation: The model that reads the retrieved context and generates the final answer. GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini, Command R+, Llama, and Mistral are common choices depending on requirements for cost, latency, accuracy, and data privacy.

Evaluation tools: Frameworks for measuring RAG performance, including retrieval accuracy (are the right chunks being found?), answer faithfulness (is the answer supported by the retrieved context?), and answer relevance (does the answer actually address the question?). RAGAS is the most widely adopted open-source evaluation framework.

Summary

Retrieval-augmented generation connects large language models to external knowledge bases, allowing them to generate answers grounded in specific, current, and verifiable information. The architecture has three stages: indexing (preparing and storing documents), retrieval (finding relevant content for each query), and generation (producing a response using the retrieved context).

RAG quality depends heavily on document preprocessing. How well your documents are parsed, structured, and chunked determines the ceiling for everything that follows. Investing in proper document AI and extraction pays dividends across the entire RAG pipeline.

The technology has matured significantly since 2020, with advanced patterns like hybrid retrieval, GraphRAG, and agentic RAG addressing many of the limitations of naive implementations. But the fundamentals remain the same: give the model the right information at the right time, and it will generate better answers than it could from training data alone.
