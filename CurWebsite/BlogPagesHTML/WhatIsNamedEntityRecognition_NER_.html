What Is Named Entity Recognition (NER)?

Named entity recognition is the process of finding specific pieces of important information in text and classifying them into predefined categories. When an NER model reads the sentence "Tesla opened a new factory in Berlin on March 15, 2026," it identifies "Tesla" as an organization, "Berlin" as a location, and "March 15, 2026" as a date.

The technology transforms unstructured text into structured data. Instead of a paragraph that a human must read and interpret, NER produces a list of labeled entities that software systems can search, filter, sort, and act on. This is what makes it possible for search engines to understand that a query about "Apple" might mean a company or a fruit, for customer support systems to extract order numbers from emails automatically, and for legal teams to find every mention of a specific party across thousands of contract pages.

NER is one of the foundational tasks in natural language processing (NLP), and it sits at the core of virtually every system that needs to pull specific facts from text, whether that text comes from a web page, a chat message, or a PDF document.

How NER Works in Simple Terms

NER operates in two steps that happen so quickly they feel like one.

Step 1: Detection. The model scans the text and identifies spans of words that might be entities. In "Dr. Sarah Chen joined Massachusetts General Hospital in September," the model detects three candidate spans: "Dr. Sarah Chen," "Massachusetts General Hospital," and "September."

Step 2: Classification. Each detected span is assigned to a category from a predefined set. "Dr. Sarah Chen" is classified as PERSON. "Massachusetts General Hospital" is classified as ORGANIZATION. "September" is classified as DATE.

The combination of detection and classification is what makes NER more than simple keyword matching. A keyword search for dates would find "September" but would also match "September" when it appears as a movie title or a band name. NER uses the surrounding context to determine that "September" in this sentence refers to a time period, not a proper noun.

Standard Entity Types

NER systems are trained to recognize a set of entity categories. The standard set used in most general-purpose models includes:

PERSON. Names of individual people. "Elon Musk," "Dr. Sarah Chen," "Judge Williams." Includes first names, last names, titles, and multi-word name combinations.

ORGANIZATION. Companies, institutions, agencies, and groups. "Tesla," "Massachusetts General Hospital," "United Nations," "Goldman Sachs." Includes abbreviations and acronyms when they refer to organizations.

LOCATION. Geographic places. "Berlin," "California," "Mount Everest," "the Pacific Ocean." Includes countries, cities, regions, landmarks, and natural features.

DATE. Calendar references. "March 15, 2026," "last Tuesday," "Q3 2025," "the fiscal year ending December 31." Includes absolute dates, relative dates, and date ranges.

TIME. Clock references. "3:30 PM," "noon," "market close." Distinct from dates because they refer to times within a day rather than calendar positions.

MONEY. Currency amounts. "$14.99," "2.5 million euros," "¥500,000." Includes the currency symbol or name and the numerical value.

PERCENTAGE. Proportional values. "15%," "three quarters," "a majority." Includes both symbolic and written forms.

QUANTITY. Measurements and counts. "500 kilograms," "three employees," "12 miles." Includes units of measure.

These eight categories cover most general-purpose NER applications. But many real-world systems extend this set with domain-specific entity types, which we will cover in the document-specific section below.

The Three Approaches to NER

NER systems have evolved through three generations of technology, each building on the limitations of the previous one.

Rule-based NER

The earliest approach uses handwritten rules and patterns to identify entities. A rule might say: "If a word starts with a capital letter and is preceded by 'Mr.' or 'Dr.,' classify it as PERSON." Another rule might say: "If a sequence of digits matches the pattern XX/XX/XXXX, classify it as DATE."

Rule-based systems are transparent (you can see exactly why each decision was made) and require no training data. But they are brittle. Every exception requires a new rule. Every new document format requires new patterns. Maintaining a large rule set becomes increasingly difficult as edge cases accumulate.

Rule-based NER still has a place in highly constrained domains where the entity formats are predictable (extracting phone numbers, email addresses, or standardized ID numbers), but it cannot handle the ambiguity and variety of natural language.

Statistical and traditional machine learning NER

The second generation uses machine learning algorithms trained on labeled text. Models like Conditional Random Fields (CRF), Hidden Markov Models (HMM), and Support Vector Machines (SVM) learn patterns from annotated training data where every word has been tagged with its entity class.

These models generalize better than rules because they learn statistical patterns rather than explicit conditions. They can handle some ambiguity by considering the context around each word. But they depend on hand-engineered features (word shape, capitalization, position in sentence, part-of-speech tags) and struggle with entities they have not seen in training.

Deep learning and transformer NER

The current generation uses neural network architectures, particularly transformers, that learn contextualized representations of every word in a sentence. Models like BERT, RoBERTa, and domain-specific variants (BioBERT for biomedical text, LegalBERT for legal text, FinBERT for financial text) process entire sentences simultaneously, allowing each word's representation to be influenced by every other word in the context.

This contextual understanding is what allows transformer-based NER to handle ambiguity correctly. In "Apple released a new iPhone," the model uses the surrounding words ("released," "iPhone") to determine that "Apple" is an organization. In "I ate an apple after lunch," the same model uses different context words ("ate," "lunch") to determine that "apple" is not an entity at all.

Transformer-based NER achieves 92 to 94% F1 scores on standard benchmarks like CoNLL-2003, which represents near-human performance on well-formatted news text. Performance varies by domain and entity type, with common entities (person names, major organizations) detected more reliably than rare or domain-specific entities.

The BIO Tagging Scheme

Under the hood, NER models do not predict entity labels directly. They predict a tag for every individual token (word or subword) in the text, using a scheme called BIO (Beginning, Inside, Outside) that handles multi-word entities.

Consider the sentence: "Dr. Sarah Chen joined Goldman Sachs."

The BIO tags would be:

Dr.     → B-PERSON    (Beginning of a PERSON entity)

Sarah   → I-PERSON    (Inside the same PERSON entity)

Chen    → I-PERSON    (Inside the same PERSON entity)

joined  → O           (Outside any entity)

Goldman → B-ORG       (Beginning of an ORG entity)

Sachs   → I-ORG       (Inside the same ORG entity)

.       → O           (Outside any entity)

The "B-" prefix marks the first token of an entity. The "I-" prefix marks continuation tokens. The "O" tag marks tokens that are not part of any entity. This scheme allows the model to handle entities of any length (from single-word entities like "Berlin" to multi-word entities like "Massachusetts General Hospital") using a fixed set of token-level predictions.

Understanding BIO tagging matters when you are preparing training data for NER models, because the annotation format must encode these token-level tags, not just highlight the entity spans.

What NER Output Looks Like

The practical output of an NER system is a list of detected entities with their positions in the source text.

json

{

  "text": "Tesla opened a new factory in Berlin on March 15, 2026.",

  "entities": [

    {

      "text": "Tesla",

      "label": "ORG",

      "start": 0,

      "end": 5,

      "confidence": 0.98

    },

    {

      "text": "Berlin",

      "label": "LOC",

      "start": 29,

      "end": 35,

      "confidence": 0.97

    },

    {

      "text": "March 15, 2026",

      "label": "DATE",

      "start": 39,

      "end": 53,

      "confidence": 0.99

    }

  ]

}

Each entity includes the extracted text, its classification label, the character-level start and end positions in the source text, and a confidence score. The character offsets allow downstream systems to locate the entity within the original text precisely, which matters for tasks like highlighting entities in a user interface or linking them back to their source context.

NER for Documents: Where Text Meets Layout

Here is where this guide diverges from every other NER introduction, and where the technology becomes most relevant to document processing.

Standard NER works on plain text. A sentence goes in, labeled entities come out. But documents are not plain text. Documents have visual structure: headers, tables, columns, form fields, and spatial relationships between elements. In a document, the meaning of a text span depends not just on the words around it but on where it appears on the page.

Consider an invoice where the text "September 15, 2026" appears in three different places:

In the header next to the word "Date:" it is the invoice date. In the line-item table under a column labeled "Ship By" it is a shipping deadline. In the footer next to "Terms: Net 30 from" it is a payment reference date.

Standard text-only NER would classify all three as DATE and consider the job done. Document NER needs to go further: it needs to classify the first as INVOICE_DATE, the second as SHIP_DATE, and the third as PAYMENT_REFERENCE_DATE. The classification depends on the spatial context (which region of the page the text appears in) as much as the textual context (which words surround it).

This is why document NER is tightly connected to document layout analysis. Layout analysis identifies the regions on the page (header, table, footer). NER identifies the entities within those regions. The combination of spatial position and textual content is what enables accurate field-level extraction from documents.

Document-specific entity types

Documents require entity categories beyond the standard PERSON/ORG/LOC/DATE set. Here are the entity types that document AI systems commonly use, organized by domain.

Financial documents: INVOICE_NUMBER, VENDOR_NAME, LINE_ITEM, UNIT_PRICE, TOTAL_AMOUNT, TAX_AMOUNT, PAYMENT_TERMS, ACCOUNT_NUMBER, ROUTING_NUMBER, FISCAL_PERIOD.

Legal documents: PARTY_NAME, EFFECTIVE_DATE, TERMINATION_DATE, CLAUSE_REFERENCE, GOVERNING_LAW, JURISDICTION, CASE_NUMBER, STATUTE_REFERENCE.

Medical documents: PATIENT_NAME, DIAGNOSIS_CODE (ICD-10), PROCEDURE_CODE (CPT), MEDICATION_NAME, DOSAGE, PROVIDER_NAME, DATE_OF_SERVICE, INSURANCE_ID.

Government forms: APPLICANT_NAME, SSN_LAST_FOUR, FILING_DATE, FORM_NUMBER, AGENCY_NAME, PERMIT_NUMBER, CASE_ID, STATUS_CODE.

Defining the right entity taxonomy for your specific document type is one of the first decisions in building a document NER system. Too few categories and the model cannot distinguish between different types of dates or amounts. Too many categories and the model cannot learn the boundaries reliably.

Multi-modal NER for documents

The most advanced document NER models process three signals simultaneously: the text content, the spatial position of each text element on the page, and the visual appearance of the document.

Models like LayoutLMv3 take text tokens, their 2D coordinates on the page, and a low-resolution image of the document as input. This multi-modal approach allows the model to learn patterns like "an amount in the bottom-right corner of a table is probably a TOTAL_AMOUNT" or "a date in the header region is probably an INVOICE_DATE or EFFECTIVE_DATE."

This spatial awareness is what separates document NER from text NER. A model that can only see text treats every date, every amount, and every name the same regardless of where it appears on the page. A model that sees both text and layout can make the same distinctions that a human reader makes by glancing at the page structure.

NER in the Document Processing Pipeline

NER does not operate in isolation. It is one stage in a larger pipeline where each stage depends on the output of the previous one.

Stage 1: Layout analysis

Document layout analysis identifies the structural regions on the page: headers, paragraphs, tables, form fields, footers. This creates the spatial context that NER uses to classify entities correctly.

Stage 2: Text extraction

OCR or text layer extraction pulls the text content from each identified region. The text is associated with its bounding box coordinates so that NER can use both the textual and spatial information.

Stage 3: Named entity recognition

NER processes the extracted text from each region, identifying and classifying entities. The spatial context from layout analysis informs the classification: an amount extracted from a "total" row in a table is tagged differently from an amount in a header.

Stage 4: Field mapping

Extracted entities are mapped to the specific fields that the downstream system expects. INVOICE_DATE maps to the "date" field in the accounting system. VENDOR_NAME maps to the "supplier" field. LINE_ITEM entities are assembled into a structured table.

Stage 5: Validation and export

Extracted fields are validated (does the date parse correctly? does the total match the sum of line items?) and exported in a structured format. This is the intelligent document processing stage where NER output becomes business-ready data.

Understanding this pipeline is important because NER accuracy depends on the quality of every upstream stage. If layout analysis misclassifies a table as body text, NER receives the wrong spatial context and may misclassify the entities within it. If OCR misreads a character, NER inherits the error.

The Ambiguity Problem

Ambiguity is the central challenge in NER. The same text can refer to completely different entity types depending on context.

"Washington" could be a person (George Washington), a city (Washington, D.C.), a state (Washington State), or part of an organization name (Washington Post).

"May" could be a month (DATE), a person's name (PERSON), or a verb ("you may proceed," which is not an entity at all).

"Amazon" could be a company (ORG), a river (LOC), or a product line (Amazon Basics).

"Apple" could be a company (ORG) or a fruit (not an entity).

Transformer-based NER models handle these ambiguities well because they process the entire sentence context before classifying any individual token. The surrounding words ("shares of Washington fell 3%" vs "flew to Washington for the summit") provide the signal needed to resolve the ambiguity.

In documents, the spatial context provides an additional disambiguation signal. "Washington" appearing in an address block is almost certainly a location. "Washington" appearing in a signatory block is almost certainly a person. "Washington" appearing in a company description could be either. The document layout gives the model evidence that pure text analysis cannot access.

Training Data for NER

NER models learn from annotated text where every entity has been labeled with its category and position. The quality, volume, and diversity of this training data directly determine the model's accuracy.

What NER training data looks like

Each training example is a text passage with entity annotations:

json

{

  "text": "Goldman Sachs reported $12.3 billion in revenue for Q3 2025.",

  "entities": [

    {"start": 0, "end": 13, "label": "ORG"},

    {"start": 23, "end": 37, "label": "MONEY"},

    {"start": 53, "end": 60, "label": "DATE"}

  ]

}

For document NER, each annotation also includes bounding box coordinates that locate the entity on the page:

json

{

  "text": "Goldman Sachs",

  "label": "VENDOR_NAME",

  "start": 0,

  "end": 13,

  "bbox": [72, 45, 220, 65],

  "page": 1

}

This spatial annotation is what makes document NER training data different from standard text NER training data, and it is why document annotation tools need to support both text labeling and spatial bounding box markup.

How much training data NER needs

The amount of training data required depends on the number and complexity of entity types.

For standard entity types (PERSON, ORG, LOC, DATE) in well-formatted text, pre-trained models like BERT can achieve good accuracy with 500 to 1,000 annotated examples through fine-tuning.

For domain-specific entity types (DIAGNOSIS_CODE, CLAUSE_REFERENCE, PERMIT_NUMBER) that the pre-trained model has never seen, you typically need 2,000 to 5,000 annotated examples per entity type to achieve production-grade accuracy.

For document NER where spatial context matters, the training data must include examples from the specific document types the model will process. An NER model fine-tuned on invoices will not automatically work on medical records because both the entity types and the layout patterns are different.

Modern automated labeling approaches can accelerate NER training data preparation by using pre-trained models to generate initial entity annotations, then having human reviewers correct the errors. AI Asset Management's DocuGraph platform applies this approach to document labeling, producing bounding box annotations and element classifications that serve as the spatial foundation for document NER pipelines.

Evaluation Metrics

NER performance is measured using three standard metrics.

Precision measures what percentage of the entities the model detected are actually correct. High precision means few false positives (the model rarely tags a non-entity as an entity).

Recall measures what percentage of the actual entities in the text the model successfully detected. High recall means few false negatives (the model rarely misses a real entity).

F1 Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a single number that balances both. An F1 of 0.93 means the model is both finding most entities and making few false predictions.

For production NER systems, the acceptable F1 score depends on the application. Information extraction for analytics can tolerate F1 scores of 0.85 to 0.90 because occasional errors average out across large volumes. Automated data entry for financial systems needs F1 above 0.95 because individual errors create downstream problems.

Accuracy varies by domain

NER accuracy is not uniform across all text types. Performance depends heavily on the domain, entity types, and text quality.

On clean news text (the CoNLL-2003 benchmark), state-of-the-art models achieve F1 scores of 92 to 94%. On biomedical text, scores drop to 85 to 90% because medical terminology is more specialized. On legal text, scores range from 80 to 90% depending on the complexity of the entity types. On financial documents, scores of 85 to 92% are typical for standard entity types.

These variations are driven by training data availability (more annotated news text exists than annotated medical text), entity type complexity (DIAGNOSIS_CODE is harder to classify than PERSON), and text quality (scanned documents with OCR errors are harder than clean digital text).

NER for RAG Systems

Retrieval-augmented generation systems are one of the fastest-growing applications of NER in 2026. The connection is practical: NER tags entities in document chunks before they are indexed, which enables more precise retrieval when users ask questions about specific people, organizations, dates, or amounts.

A RAG system without NER treats each document chunk as a bag of words. A user asking "What was Goldman Sachs' revenue in Q3 2025?" triggers a semantic similarity search that may return chunks mentioning revenue, Goldman Sachs, or Q3, but the system cannot guarantee that the returned chunk mentions all three in the same context.

A RAG system with NER tags each chunk with its entities during indexing. The retrieval system can then filter for chunks that contain both a VENDOR_NAME entity matching "Goldman Sachs" and a DATE entity matching "Q3 2025" before applying semantic similarity, dramatically improving the precision of retrieved results.

Common Challenges

Nested entities. "Bank of America headquarters in Charlotte" contains a nested structure: "Bank of America" is an ORG, "Charlotte" is a LOC, and "America" is part of the ORG name but also a LOC in other contexts. Standard BIO tagging cannot represent nested entities, which requires either a modified tagging scheme or a span-based prediction approach.

Rare and emerging entities. NER models struggle with entities they have not seen in training data: new company names, recently formed organizations, or newly coined terminology. Few-shot and zero-shot techniques help, but rare entities remain the hardest category for any NER system.

Cross-lingual entities. Documents containing entities in multiple languages (a contract mentioning both "Société Générale" and "Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi UFJ") challenge models trained primarily on English text. Multilingual transformer models (XLM-RoBERTa) improve cross-lingual NER but still underperform monolingual models.

Long documents. Transformer models have context window limits (typically 512 to 4,096 tokens). Documents that exceed this limit must be processed in segments, and entities that span segment boundaries may be missed or split. Sliding window approaches with overlap partially address this.

Inconsistent training data. When annotators disagree on entity boundaries ("New York City" vs "New York" + "City") or classifications ("Goldman Sachs Group" as one ORG vs "Goldman Sachs" as ORG + "Group" as non-entity), the model learns inconsistent patterns that reduce accuracy on all similar entities.

Getting Started With NER

If you are building a system that needs to extract named entities from text or documents, here is a practical path forward.

For plain text NER, start with a pre-trained transformer model. HuggingFace's token classification pipeline provides production-ready NER with a few lines of Python. SpaCy's NER component is another accessible option with good documentation and community support.

For document NER, start with layout analysis to identify document regions, then apply NER within each region. The spatial context from layout analysis is what enables accurate field-level extraction. Our complete guide to document AI covers the full pipeline architecture.

For custom entity types, plan to fine-tune a pre-trained model on your own annotated data. The standard PERSON/ORG/LOC/DATE categories are well-handled by off-the-shelf models. Domain-specific entities (invoice numbers, medical codes, legal references) require training data from your specific domain.

For production deployment, monitor entity-level accuracy metrics continuously. NER performance can degrade over time as new entity names emerge and document formats change. A feedback loop where human corrections on production errors feed back into training data keeps the model current.

Summary

Named entity recognition identifies and classifies specific pieces of information in text, transforming unstructured language into structured data that software systems can search, filter, and act on. The technology has evolved from brittle rule-based systems through statistical models to transformer architectures that achieve near-human accuracy on well-formatted text.

For document processing, NER gains an additional dimension: spatial context. In a document, the meaning of an entity depends on where it appears on the page as much as what words surround it. This connection between NER and document layout analysis is what enables accurate field extraction from invoices, contracts, medical records, and government forms.

The accuracy of any NER system depends on its training data. Standard entity types are well-served by pre-trained models. Domain-specific entities require annotated examples from your specific document types and use cases. Getting the training data right, with consistent annotations, representative diversity, and accurate boundary definitions, is the single highest-leverage investment in NER accuracy.
