How to Label PDF Documents for Machine Learning Training

If you are building a document AI model, the quality of your labeled PDF data determines whether your model works in production or fails on real documents. This is not an exaggeration. Research consistently shows that training data quality has a larger impact on model accuracy than model architecture, hyperparameter tuning, or training duration.

Yet most guides on data labeling treat PDFs as an afterthought. They explain how to label images or text, then mention that "documents can also be labeled" without addressing the specific challenges that PDF labeling involves: coordinate systems that differ between PDF and image formats, text layers that may or may not exist, multi-page documents that need page-level processing, and structural elements (tables, headers, footers) that require spatial annotation alongside classification.

This guide walks through the complete workflow for labeling PDF documents for machine learning, from the moment you have a folder of unlabeled PDFs to the moment you have a structured training dataset loaded into PyTorch, TensorFlow, or HuggingFace.

What "Labeling a PDF" Actually Means

When we talk about labeling a PDF for machine learning, we mean creating a structured record that describes every region on every page of the document. For each region, the record includes:

A classification label that identifies what type of element the region is: header, paragraph, table, figure, list item, footer, caption, or another category from your chosen taxonomy.

A bounding box that defines the spatial boundaries of the region on the page. Bounding boxes are specified as four coordinates, typically [x1, y1, x2, y2], where (x1, y1) is the top-left corner and (x2, y2) is the bottom-right corner of the rectangle enclosing the element.

The text content extracted from the region (when the element contains readable text).

Optional metadata including confidence scores (for auto-generated labels), page dimensions, and document-level information.

The combination of classification labels and spatial coordinates is what makes PDF labeling different from plain text labeling. In text labeling, you tag words or sentences with categories. In PDF labeling, you tag visual regions of a page with both categories and coordinates, because the model needs to learn not just what each element is but where it is on the page.

This spatial component is what powers document layout analysis models, which need bounding box training data to learn where headers, tables, and paragraphs appear across different document layouts.

Step 1: Prepare Your PDF Collection

Before you label anything, organize the documents you will work with.

Assess your document diversity

Look at the PDFs you plan to label and answer these questions:

How many different document types are represented (invoices, contracts, reports, forms)? How many different sources produced them (different vendors, different departments, different software)? How much layout variation exists within each type (do all your invoices look the same, or does every vendor use a different format)?

The answers determine how much labeled data you need. A single document type from a single source with consistent formatting may need only 200 to 500 labeled pages for a well-performing model. Diverse documents from many sources may need 2,000 to 5,000 labeled pages or more.

Separate digital-native from scanned PDFs

Digital-native PDFs (created in Word, InDesign, or other software) contain an embedded text layer with precise character positions. This text layer makes labeling faster and more accurate because the text content of each region can be extracted directly rather than requiring OCR.

Scanned PDFs are images of paper documents. They have no text layer. Every character must be recognized through OCR before text content can be associated with labeled regions. Scanned documents also introduce quality issues (noise, skew, faded text) that affect labeling accuracy.

Separate these two types in your collection because they require different preprocessing and produce different levels of labeling accuracy. Include both types in your training data if your production system will encounter both.

Set aside an evaluation set

Before labeling begins, hold out 10 to 15% of your documents as a test set that will never be used for training. These held-out documents should represent the full diversity of your production documents, including the most challenging layouts and the lowest-quality scans. You will use this set to measure your model's real-world accuracy after training.

Do not select the cleanest, easiest documents for your test set. Select documents that represent what your model will actually encounter, including the hard cases.

Step 2: Define Your Label Taxonomy

The taxonomy is the set of categories you will use to classify document regions. This decision shapes everything that follows, from annotation guidelines to model capabilities to downstream extraction accuracy.

Start with standard categories

Begin with the element types that appear in virtually all documents:

page_header for repeated text at the top of pages (document titles, running headers). section_header for titles that introduce new content sections. paragraph (or text) for blocks of body content. table for structured grid content with rows and columns. figure for images, charts, diagrams, and other visual content. list_item for individual items in bulleted, numbered, or lettered lists. caption for short text describing an adjacent figure or table. page_footer for repeated text at the bottom of pages. page_number for standalone page number elements.

These nine categories follow the conventions used by major public datasets like PubLayNet (5 categories), DocBank (12 categories), and DocLayNet (11 categories). Using standard category names ensures your labeled data is compatible with pre-trained models from these datasets.

Add domain-specific categories when needed

If your application requires finer-grained distinctions than the standard categories provide, add domain-specific labels. A legal document labeling project might add "clause," "definition," and "signature_block." A financial document project might add "line_item," "subtotal_row," and "disclosure_note."

Add categories only when your application genuinely needs the distinction. Every additional category increases the complexity of the labeling task and requires more training examples for the model to learn the new boundaries reliably. Our guide to choosing a labeling taxonomy covers the tradeoffs in detail.

Write boundary rules for ambiguous cases

For every pair of categories that could be confused, write an explicit rule that resolves the ambiguity. Bold text between two paragraphs: header or emphasized body text? Define the rule: "Bold text on its own line with whitespace above and below is section_header. Bold text within a paragraph is paragraph." A short text block next to a figure: caption or paragraph? Define the rule: "Text within 20 points below or beside a figure that describes the figure is caption. All other adjacent text is paragraph."

Write these rules before labeling starts. Annotators will encounter every ambiguous case you can anticipate, and many you cannot. The rules you write upfront prevent the inconsistencies that degrade model accuracy.

Step 3: Choose Your Labeling Approach

You have three options for generating labels, and the right choice depends on your volume, budget, and accuracy requirements.

Manual labeling

A human annotator opens each PDF page, draws bounding boxes around every structural element, and classifies each region from the taxonomy. This produces the highest-quality labels because every decision involves human judgment. It also takes the longest: 3 to 8 minutes per page, depending on document complexity.

Manual labeling makes sense for small projects (under 500 pages), for complex documents where automated tools underperform, and for creating the gold-standard evaluation data that you measure all other approaches against.

Automated labeling

A pre-trained model processes the PDF and generates bounding boxes, classifications, and confidence scores automatically. Processing takes seconds per page rather than minutes. The auto-generated labels are typically 90%+ accurate on standard document layouts, with lower accuracy on unusual formats, scanned documents, and complex table structures.

Automated labeling makes sense for large projects where manual annotation would take weeks, for documents with standard layouts where the model's accuracy is sufficient, and for generating initial labels that human reviewers then refine.

Hybrid labeling (recommended)

The hybrid approach combines automated and manual labeling. An automated tool generates initial labels for every page. A human reviewer then corrects the small percentage of predictions that are wrong, adjusts bounding boxes that are imprecise, and adds labels for elements the model missed.

This approach produces the same label quality as full manual annotation at a fraction of the time. The automated step handles the 90% of elements that are straightforward. The human step handles the 10% that require judgment. A page that takes 5 minutes to label manually takes 15 to 30 seconds to auto-label and 20 to 30 seconds to review and correct.

Step 4: Label Your Documents

Regardless of which approach you chose, the labeling process follows the same structural workflow for each page.

Render the page

If you are labeling manually or using a tool that processes page images, each PDF page needs to be rendered as an image at a consistent resolution. 200 to 300 DPI is the standard range. Higher DPI preserves more visual detail but increases processing time and storage requirements. Lower DPI saves resources but may lose fine text and thin table borders.

If you are using a tool that processes native PDF structure (like DocuGraph), the tool handles rendering internally and you work with the original PDF directly.

Identify all regions on the page

Every distinct structural element should receive its own bounding box and label. This includes the obvious elements (headers, paragraphs, tables) and the easily overlooked ones (page numbers, footnotes, captions, small logos).

Missing elements are a common labeling error. A page where the footer is not labeled teaches the model that footers do not exist. In production, the model will either ignore footers (missing them entirely) or misclassify them as body text (contaminating extracted content with repeated footer text).

Draw precise bounding boxes

Bounding boxes should tightly enclose each element with consistent, minimal padding. Not so tight that text is clipped at the edges. Not so loose that adjacent elements overlap. Consistent padding across all labeled pages is more important than perfect pixel alignment, because inconsistent padding teaches the model noisy boundary patterns.

For table elements, draw the bounding box around the entire table including its header row and any border lines. Do not draw separate bounding boxes for individual cells at this stage (cell-level annotation is a separate task that requires dedicated table structure training data).

Assign classification labels

Apply labels from your taxonomy to every bounding box. Follow the boundary rules you defined in Step 2 for ambiguous cases. If you encounter an element type that your taxonomy does not cover, note it for a taxonomy revision rather than forcing it into an existing category.

Handle multi-page documents

Process each page independently. Each page in your labeled dataset should be a self-contained training example with its own dimensions, its own element list, and its own bounding boxes. Multi-page documents produce multiple training examples.

Be aware that some elements span page boundaries (a table that starts on page 3 and continues on page 4). Label each page's portion of the table as a separate "table" element. The model will learn to detect table regions on each page independently.

Step 5: Understand Your Coordinate System

The coordinate system used for bounding boxes is one of the most common sources of bugs in document AI pipelines. Getting this wrong produces labeled data where the spatial information does not match what the model expects, causing training to fail silently (the model trains but learns wrong spatial patterns).

PDF points

The native coordinate system of PDF documents. The origin is at the top-left (in most modern tools) or bottom-left (in the PDF specification). 72 points per inch. A US Letter page is 612 x 792 points. This is the most common format for document AI training data because it matches the source format.

Image pixels

The coordinate system of rendered page images. The origin is at the top-left. Coordinates scale with the rendering DPI. The same element has different pixel coordinates at 200 DPI versus 300 DPI. If your labels use pixel coordinates, you must record the DPI alongside the page dimensions so the model can normalize the coordinates.

Normalized coordinates (0 to 1)

Each coordinate is expressed as a fraction of the page dimension. [0.12, 0.06, 0.88, 0.09] means the box spans from 12% to 88% of the page width and 6% to 9% of the page height. This format is DPI-independent but introduces floating-point precision issues.

The critical rule

Use one coordinate system consistently across your entire dataset. Document which system you used in the dataset metadata. If your model expects a different coordinate system than your labels use, write the conversion in your data loader, not in your labeling process. Keep the source-of-truth labels clean and convert at load time.

Step 6: Export Your Labeled Data

The standard export format for labeled PDF data is structured JSON. Each page produces one JSON record containing the page dimensions, the list of labeled elements, and the bounding box coordinates for each element.

{

  "file": "invoice_042.pdf",

  "page": 1,

  "width": 612,

  "height": 792,

  "elements": [

    {

      "id": "p1_e1",

      "label": "page_header",

      "bbox": [72, 30, 540, 55],

      "text": "Acme Corporation",

      "confidence": 0.97

    },

    {

      "id": "p1_e2",

      "label": "table",

      "bbox": [72, 200, 540, 450],

      "confidence": 0.93

    },

    {

      "id": "p1_e3",

      "label": "page_footer",

      "bbox": [72, 750, 540, 780],

      "text": "Page 1 of 3",

      "confidence": 0.96

    }

  ]

}

For large datasets, use JSONL (JSON Lines) format where each line is one page's record. JSONL files load efficiently into PyTorch, TensorFlow, and HuggingFace without parsing the entire file into memory.

The export should include confidence scores if your labels were generated by an automated tool. This enables confidence-based filtering during model training: you can train on high-confidence labels only, then progressively add lower-confidence examples as your model improves.

Step 7: Validate Before Training

Before feeding your labeled data into a training pipeline, run validation checks that catch the most common quality issues.

Label distribution check

Count how many examples of each label type appear in your dataset. If "table" appears 500 times but "caption" appears only 12 times, the model will learn tables well and captions poorly. You may need to add more examples of underrepresented categories or adjust your loss function to weight rare classes more heavily.

Bounding box sanity check

Verify that no bounding boxes extend beyond the page boundaries. Check that no bounding boxes have zero or negative dimensions. Identify bounding boxes that overlap significantly with other bounding boxes on the same page (which may indicate duplicate or conflicting labels).

Consistency check

If multiple annotators labeled your data, select a subset of pages that were labeled by different annotators and compare their labels. Measure inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's kappa) to quantify how consistently the taxonomy was applied. Low agreement on specific categories indicates that the boundary rules for those categories need clarification.

Visual spot check

Open 20 to 30 random pages from your labeled dataset in a visual editor that displays the bounding boxes over the original PDF. Visually verify that the boxes are positioned correctly, the labels make sense, and no elements were missed. This manual check catches systematic errors that automated validation misses.

Step 8: Load Into Your ML Framework

The final step is loading your labeled data into the framework you will use for model training.

PyTorch

import json

from torch.utils.data import Dataset

class DocumentLayoutDataset(Dataset):

    def __init__(self, jsonl_path):

        with open(jsonl_path) as f:

            self.pages = [json.loads(line) for line in f]

    def __len__(self):

        return len(self.pages)

    def __getitem__(self, idx):

        page = self.pages[idx]

        return {

            "boxes": [e["bbox"] for e in page["elements"]],

            "labels": [e["label"] for e in page["elements"]],

            "width": page["width"],

            "height": page["height"]

        }

import json

import tensorflow as tf

def page_generator(jsonl_path):

    with open(jsonl_path) as f:

        for line in f:

            page = json.loads(line)

            for element in page["elements"]:

                yield {

                    "bbox": element["bbox"],

                    "label": element["label"]

                }

dataset = tf.data.Dataset.from_generator(

    lambda: page_generator("train.jsonl"),

        "bbox": tf.TensorSpec(shape=(4,), dtype=tf.float32),

        "label": tf.TensorSpec(shape=(), dtype=tf.string)

    }

Step 9: Iterate on Your Labels

The first training run on your labeled data will not produce a perfect model. That is expected. What matters is what you do next.

After training, evaluate the model on your held-out test set. Examine the errors. They will cluster around specific patterns: a category that the model consistently confuses with another, a document layout that the model has not seen enough examples of, or a bounding box pattern where the model's predictions are systematically too large or too small.

Each error pattern points back to a specific issue in the training data. Fix the issue (relabel the inconsistent pages, add more examples of the underrepresented layout, correct the bounding box padding), retrain, and evaluate again.

This iterative cycle, where model errors inform label corrections and label corrections improve the model, is the core practice of data-centric AI. The teams that iterate on their labels as aggressively as they iterate on their code consistently build the best-performing document AI models.

Summary

Labeling PDF documents for machine learning is a nine-step process: prepare your PDF collection, define your label taxonomy, choose your labeling approach (manual, automated, or hybrid), label your documents with bounding boxes and classifications, understand your coordinate system, export as structured JSON, validate label quality, load into your ML framework, and iterate based on model errors.

The quality of your labeled data determines the ceiling of your model's performance. No model architecture or training technique can compensate for inconsistent labels, missing elements, imprecise bounding boxes, or a training distribution that does not match your production documents. Getting the labels right is the single most impactful investment you can make in a document AI project.
